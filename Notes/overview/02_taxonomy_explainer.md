## Scale

**8 groups · 30 sub-groups · more than 160 functions**, in a nested hierarchy
`Group → Sub-group → Function`.

## The worked example

| | |
|---|---|
| Organism | Namib desert beetle |
| Challenge | capture water in a very arid climate |
| Strategy | the wing covers gather water from the air using **nanoscale bumps and body position** |
| Function | to capture liquid |
| **Group** | Get, store, or distribute resources |
| **Sub-group** | Capture, absorb, or filter resources |
| **Function** | Capture, absorb, or filter **liquids** |

That is the exact shape our model table needs: organism → challenge → strategy → function →
taxonomy path.

## Three ways to query the Taxonomy

Their example challenge is *"a building in an area of low rainfall that must capture and
store rainwater"* — near enough to our F6–F8 to copy the method directly.

1. **Direct verbs.** Identify the verbs that define the challenge. → *How does nature capture
   water? Store water?*
2. **Related concepts.** Go beyond the exact challenge to related functions with possibly
   similar solutions. → *How does nature absorb water? Capture fog? Manage humidity? Move
   water?*
3. **The inverse.** Turn the question around — *"sometimes asking the opposite of your
   original question can provide new insights"*. → *How does nature remove water? Stay dry?*

Applied to us, approach 3 is the interesting one. Inverting our functions gives:
*How does nature keep heat in?* (insulation, winter), *How does nature stay dry?* (our F10
and the lichen strategy), *How does nature avoid losing water?* (our F7 metering problem
seen from the other side — desert plants are champions at exactly this).

## Two entry points on AskNature

- **Search bar** — ask the question directly: *"How does nature stay dry?"*
- **Function menu** — the top level of the Taxonomy is linked from the homepage; clicking a
  top-level function opens the whole Taxonomy with the content attached to each function.
