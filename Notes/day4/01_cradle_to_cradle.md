# Cradle to Cradle — lecture notes

Source: `Cradle to Cradle.pptx`, 38 slides. Braungart and McDonough's concept, taught from
the material side.

## Why this lecture exists

Slide 2 is the Section 4 brief, quoted:

> - Life's Principles most relevant to your final concept, and **assess how well the concept
>   (V2) actually applies them**
> - Discuss environmental benefits **as well as limitations and trade-offs**. Avoid
>   unsupported claims such as "sustainable" or "resource efficient"
> - Where relevant, discuss **material use, durability, repair, reuse, modularity,
>   recyclability** and/or Cradle-to-Cradle / Circular Economy aspects
> - Explain **whether sustainability considerations led to changes in the design**

The last point is the one we can fail silently: if nothing in our concept changed because of
a sustainability consideration, Section 4 reads as an add-on. It is worth deciding now what
did change.

## The linear economy and why it persists

| | Linear economy |
|---|---|
| Resources / products | take, make, waste — *cradle to grave* |
| Mechanism | economies of scale, design to cost, low-cost inputs |
| Role of recycling | a form of disposal, i.e. a waste problem |
| Impact | resource depletion, emissions |

The explanation the lecture gives for why this persists is a business one, and it matters
for a retrofit product: in a **sales model** the manufacturer hands over ownership, so the
value created by circular design accrues to somebody else. The recycler benefits from
recyclable materials, the user from durability, the service technician from repairability —
**the manufacturer captures none of it**, and therefore has no incentive to invest.

Consequence named on slide 9: producers have no incentive to invest in durability or
repairability, and cannot manage sourcing risk because they have no access to the material
in disposed products. The remedy is a different business model — deposit, leasing, or
service.

## Eco-efficiency against eco-effectiveness

The distinction the whole lecture turns on, with the brake-pad example from slide 20:

| Approach | The brake pad |
|---|---|
| **Linear** | minimise cost, maximise sales, dump toxic material |
| **Eco-efficient** — *less bad* | emit fewer particles, but still thousands of tonnes in total |
| **Eco-effective** — *actively good* | make it from a material that can safely return to a biological cycle |

> "Products should not be *less bad* but **actively good**." (slide 26)

And the underlying image: nature is **not efficient**. A cherry tree produces thousands of
blossoms wastefully — but every one becomes a nutrient. It is *effective* because it is part
of a system that reuses everything. **Waste is food.**

**Where our project stands.** Reducing absorbed radiation is eco-efficiency: a less bad
façade. There is one part of our concept that is eco-effective in the strict sense, and we
should say so precisely rather than broadly: the water path takes **rainwater the city
currently discards into the sewer** and turns it into cooling. That is waste becoming food,
and it is the only claim of this kind we can make honestly.

## The two cycles, and the rule that assigns a layer to one of them

- **Biological nutrients** — consumer goods that are worn away in use and must be able to
  return safely to the environment: cleaning agents, shampoos, packaging.
- **Technical nutrients** — durable goods that are *used* rather than consumed: cars,
  washing machines. They provide a service and their components are recovered.

The rule, from slide 27, is unusually crisp:

> **Anything that is consumed (worn out) has to come from the biological cycle. Anything
> that is just used (without wear) can come from the technical nutrient cycle.**

**Applied to our build-up this decides the material strategy layer by layer**, and it gives
Section 4 a structure it does not currently have:

| Layer | Worn or used? | Cycle it belongs in |
|---|---|---|
| Outer coating or finish | **worn** — UV, driving rain, freeze–thaw, soiling | biological, or at minimum benign in breakdown |
| Water-carrying absorbent layer | worn, and in direct contact with run-off | biological |
| Ventilated cavity, sub-structure, fixings | **used**, not consumed | technical — recoverable, demountable |
| Masonry behind | untouched | outside the product |

That table is worth building properly in Section 4. Note what it implies: **the layer we are
most tempted to engineer chemically is the one that must be biologically benign**, because it
is the one that wears away into the street.

## The eight cycle design principles (slide 35)

1. No use of ecotoxic chemicals
2. Recyclable materials
3. Easy to dismantle, modular construction
4. Durability
5. Simple maintainability and reparability
6. Cascade use of raw materials
7. Planned return logistics
8. Efficient use of energy

Eight is a usable checklist for Section 4 — with the caveat that a checklist ticked without
evidence is exactly what the rubric marks down. Two of the eight we can already answer with
something concrete: **4 durability** through KPI 7, and **8 energy** through the hard
constraint that the cooling function consumes none. **7 return logistics** is the one we have
never considered and would have to invent.

## Rohner Textiles — the worked example

A Swiss textile mill facing closure in the 1980s over dye pollution. The redesign kept
**16 dyes out of 1600**, chose fibres that are essentially edible, and made the fabric
compostable as fertiliser. The result the lecture likes to quote: the water leaving the
factory was **cleaner than the water entering it** — checked by the St. Gallen environment
agency, which first suspected broken instruments. The line continues today as Climatex, under
Gessner Textil in Wädenswil.

The transferable point for us is not the textile. It is that the environmental result came
from **radical subtraction of the chemical palette**, not from a treatment stage bolted on
the end.
