# Day 4 background reading — what is usable

Three documents, two of them long. Both Ellen MacArthur Foundation texts are the same work:
*Delivering the Circular Economy — A Toolkit for Policymakers*, with Denmark as the worked
case. The Denmark file is the case study; the toolkit is the method with the same chapters
repeated.

## Construction and the built environment (EMF, Denmark case study pp. 54–61)

The relevant chapter for us, and it contains figures our own claims currently lack.

**Where the waste is.** Almost **75 % of the average cost of a new house comes from the
construction process** itself. Two forms of resource inefficiency follow from fragmented
construction, maintenance and renovation — multiple stakeholders, no full project oversight,
traditional on-site technique:

- heavy reliance on **virgin, finite materials assembled manually on site**;
- **10–15 % of materials wasted on site**, through over-ordering, inadequate storage, theft
  and poor coordination.

**That second figure is the one to use.** Our Life's Principles section claims *built to
shape* — a modular element sized to a façade grid produces no offcuts — but claims it without
a number. Ten to fifteen per cent is the number, from a citable source, and it turns a
plausible-sounding principle into a quantified one.

**The three opportunities** the report identifies for construction and real estate:

1. **Industrialised production, modularisation and 3D printing** — reduce build time and
   structural waste, if technology develops and traditional industry habits are overcome.
2. **Reuse and high-quality recycling of components and materials** — reduce demand for new
   materials, *if the split incentives created by a fragmented market are addressed*.
3. **Sharing, multi-purposing and repurposing of buildings** — reduce demand for new
   buildings through better use of existing floor space.

Opportunity 1 is ours directly: a prefabricated, grid-sized retrofit element is exactly the
industrialised, off-site route the report advocates. Opportunity 2 names the obstacle we
would hit — the split incentive between owner, tenant and city.

**Building examples worth naming in Section 4:**

- **Park 20/20**, Netherlands, developed by Delta Development — a C2C-designed office complex
  **built for disassembly**, with asset tracking so components can be recovered later. The
  closest built precedent to "designed for disassembly" as a real project rather than a
  principle.
- **Upcycle House**, Denmark — built from processed recycled materials, reported to reduce
  overall CO₂ emissions by **86 %** against a benchmark house.
- **One and Two Potomac Yard**, US EPA, Arlington — built with **27 % recycled content**,
  including slag concrete aggregate, fly ash and gypsum wallboard.
- **Rockwool** — an insulation manufacturer incorporating recycled industrial material, i.e.
  the material class closest to what our build-up would need.

## Interface — *Lessons for the Future*, 25 years of Mission Zero

Interface set out in 1994 to reach **zero negative impact by 2020** — announced by a carpet
tile manufacturer that, in its own words, had never thought about the environment. The
document is structured as nine lessons, of which two are worth borrowing for how we write
Section 4 and Section 5:

- **"A wrong turn can lead to the right result"** — a company narrative that keeps its dead
  ends visible. That is the same move our de-selection tables make.
- **"Be transparent"** — publishing the numbers including the unflattering ones.

Interface already appears in the Day 2 material as a biomimicry case (carpet tiles modelled
on a forest floor, so no two tiles need match and single tiles can be replaced). Between the
two, it is the one company we can follow from biological inspiration through to material
strategy — useful if Section 4 needs a single coherent example rather than five fragments.

## What not to do with these documents

Both EMF texts are **policy documents**, written for governments, and most of their content
is about instruments: taxes, targets, procurement, regulation. That is outside our project
boundary and outside the report's scope. Take the construction chapter and the figures; leave
the policy recommendations alone.
