# Circular economy measures — lecture notes

Source: `Slides Circular economy part II.pdf` (Baumgartner), 36 slides.

## The framing: circularity is itself a biomimicry lesson

> "Materials are kept circular in nature, by reusing or repurposing, perfectly balanced —
> another strategy of nature at system level." (slide 2, with the carbon, phosphorus and
> nitrogen cycles as the examples)

Three lessons from nature for a circular economy (slide 3):

1. **Renew and regenerate** — cycle resources back into new or existing systems.
2. **Protect and extend** — organisms protect themselves and their environment.
3. **Turn even the most challenging waste into value** — *"the concept of waste doesn't
   really exist in nature"*.

The slide then asks us directly: where are the links to the Life's Principles, and what
applies to our solution? Point 3 is the one we can answer — see the rainwater argument in
[`01_cradle_to_cradle.md`](01_cradle_to_cradle.md).

## The Ellen MacArthur measures

From the butterfly diagram, the four sources of value:

- **Power of the inner circle** — the tighter the loop, the more of the embedded value
  survives. Repair beats refurbishment beats recycling.
- **Power of circling longer** — more cycles, or longer cycles.
- **Power of cascaded use** — a material moving into successive lower-grade uses.
- **Power of pure, non-toxic or at least easier-to-separate inputs** — purity at the input
  end is what makes any of the above possible at the output end.

The last one is the one that binds us: **a build-up glued together from three materials
cannot be separated**, however recyclable each material is on its own. Our Life's Principles
section already claims *designed for disassembly*; this is the argument behind it.

Retaining value is presented twice: as the **resource diamond** for the technological cycle
and the **resource leaf** for the biological cycle, both integrating the waste hierarchy with
the R-principles.

## The warning that matters most to us

Slide 14, quoted, because it is the sharpest thing in the day:

> "Practitioners often regard the circular economy as a concept based on one simple rule:
> the more material is kept in circulation, the better. **However, the mere fact that
> materials are circulated does not necessarily equate to greater sustainability.**"

The counter-example: an additive in plastics that is harmless in its first application — a
laptop case — and **hazardous in the second, after recycling into a children's toy**. The
lecture explicitly ties this back to the Life's Principle *use life-friendly chemistry*.

**This is our biocide argument arriving from the material side.** We reason that a biocide
must not leach into rainwater we then evaporate at head height. The circular-economy version
is the same failure one step later: a substance that is acceptable where it starts and
unacceptable where the cycle takes it. Section 4 should make that connection explicitly —
the same rule, reached from two directions, is a stronger argument than either alone.

## How to check toxicity, concretely

The lecture names tools rather than leaving it abstract, and these are directly usable for
whatever coating or binder our concept ends up proposing:

- **REACH / ECHA** — <https://chem.echa.europa.eu/> — registration, evaluation, authorisation
  and restriction of chemicals in the EU. Worked through with Bisphenol A as the example.
- **PubChem** — <https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/>
- **EU RISCTOX**, via EU-OSHA
- **Chemical Safety (SDS)** — <https://chemicalsafety.com/sds-search/>
- **EPA DSSTox** and **ToxRefDB**

A short paragraph in Section 4 saying which substance we checked, in which database, and
what we found would be worth more than a page of general sustainability language.

## Circular business models

Four, from slide 21 — the point being that the ownership question decides who benefits from
durability:

| Model | Who owns it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rent and leasing** | provider | car rental |
| **Service** | provider | *illuminated surface* sold instead of lamps — the provider supplies the lamps, the electricity and the maintenance |
| **Deposit** | sold, with a deposit on the product or one component | a deposit on the motor block |
| Sale | customer | the default, and the one with no circular incentive |

**For us this is out of scope and should stay out** — Section 2 places the business model
outside the project boundary. But it is worth one sentence in Section 4 or 5, because the
split-incentive problem is real for a façade retrofit: the owner pays, the tenant benefits
from comfort, the city benefits from the street, and nobody owns the outcome. A service model
— *cooling delivered rather than panels sold* — is the standard answer, and naming it shows
we know why the boundary was drawn where it was.

## The leading questions we are expected to answer (slide 35)

- Do we circulate substances and materials in **technical or biological** cycles?
- How is the product designed for **modularity, circularity and non-toxic materials** in
  those two cycles?
- Which measures **prolong use** — cascades, repairability, long-lasting materials?
- What are the potential **recirculation logistics**?
- Which **business model** — service, leasing, deposit?
- How do the solutions fit the planned functionality and increase the degree of innovation?

Four of the six we can answer today. **Recirculation logistics** and **business model** we
cannot, and the honest move is to say so and say why, rather than inventing them.
