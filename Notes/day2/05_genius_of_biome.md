# Genius of Biome — temperate broadleaf forest (HOK + Biomimicry 3.8)

Source: `Background reading Genius of Biome Report FINAL.pdf`, 178 pages, © 2011–2013
Biomimicry Group / HOK.

## Why this document is our best background reading

It is a **local-lens document for our exact biome**. Zurich sits in the temperate broadleaf
forest biome, and the report was written specifically to translate that biome's strategies
into the **built environment**, with design principles and application sketches per
strategy. Chapters: Water (p. 27) · Energy (p. 59) · Materials (p. 97) · Social (p. 111) ·
Economic (p. 143).

Its structure per strategy is exactly the abstraction the lecture asks for:
**nature's design → design principle → application ideas ("BaDT brainstorm")**.

## Energy chapter (p. 59) — "adjust to temperature change"

The chapter's own framing (p. 59):

> "A common summer condition in the temperate broadleaf forest is high heat combined with
> high humidity. Plants have an elegant cooling system that is coupled to production of
> energy and the passive processes of evaporation, water adhesion, and cohesion. […]
> Humidity is a resource that is used to help cool every leaf, and in turn, the whole
> forest. […] nature rarely spends energy on resistance and makes use of locally available
> resources. The other pattern can be described as the principle of **'lots of littles'** —
> many small, seemingly weak elements combine to create a powerful, problem-solving
> process."

Function list of the chapter: *cool structures · move moisture passively · reduce energy
expenditure · create thermal cover · store energy · optimize energy sources.*

### Leaf: "passive valve cools with moisture" (p. 62–64) — maps to our F6–F9

Nature's design:
- Transpiration pulls a string of water molecules from the roots up through the plant via
  **cohesion and adhesion**.
- Water is drawn to the **guard cells** of the stomata by osmosis (potassium pump).
- Guard cells swell with water and contract on loss of it; this **opens and closes the
  pore**.
- The top surface is sealed by **wax-coated, interlocking pavement cells**; stomata sit in
  greater numbers on the **underside**.
- **"Leaf surfaces can cool by as much as 11.1 to 16.7 °C."**
- **"The average temperature in urban areas is 8 °C warmer in the summer than in the
  dense, tall forested vegetation surrounding them."** — an independent statement of our
  problem, from the biome side.

Design principle (p. 63), quoted because it is close to a specification for our concept:

> "Design an evaporative cooling system that draws moisture from a source and delivers it
> to a sink site where **valve-like pores are triggered to open and close based on humidity
> and heat levels**. A passive energy pump draws water from a lower source through a
> structure to the outside of that structure to create a water vapor layer that cools the
> whole structure. […] **Many units of evaporation produce an additive effect.**"

Related design principles listed: moisture flow responds to varying requirements ·
repeated structures create an additive effect · systems are coupled to reinforce effect ·
valve opens and closes in response to humidity and heat fluctuations · different forms of
water (vapour and liquid) allow the cooling effect · **valve positioned away from the heat
source to protect from desiccation**.

Application ideas (p. 64) — two are directly usable:
- Evaporative cooling is normally dismissed as ineffective in humid climates, **"yet this
  is the cooling method of choice for all vegetation in this habitat"**. That is the
  counter-argument to the standard objection against an evaporative façade in Switzerland.
- The water moving through a plant is cool because the earth holds it near 10 °C — could
  ground-cooled water replace a refrigerant?
- **Valves tuned to ambient temperature and humidity** — i.e. F9 realised as a valve rather
  than as a material property.

Page 65 sketches a **"BIO-ADAPTIVE FACADE"** with a transpirator panel (liquid-desiccant
cooling: moist air + solar heat + calcium chloride), a solar hot-water panel, a boundary
layer and misting from collected water. Worth reproducing as prior art in Section 3.2.

### Trees: "transpiration moves water" (p. 68–70) — maps to F3, F6–F8

- Cohesion and adhesion overcome gravity; evaporation at the leaf creates the **pressure
  gradient** that pulls water from soil (high water potential) to leaf (low). No pump.
- Scale ladder: leaf → tree → forest → ecosystem. Our equivalent ladder is
  surface → façade → street canyon → district, and it is the same argument we make about
  KPI 3 vs KPI 4.

Application ideas (p. 70):
- **"Create a boundary layer on roof surfaces that can be opened and closed"** — extra
  insulation in winter, cooling airflow in summer. This is the seasonal-switching idea
  again, realised geometrically instead of optically.
- Use air movement / prevailing wind to increase evaporative cooling.
- Capillary transport up a building, then recover energy on the way down.

### Animals in winter: "thermal cover protects from cold" (p. 80–82)

- Insulation that **adjusts**: birds fluff feathers to capture air; mammals grow thicker
  underfur under waterproof guard hairs; insulating structures "can adjust to temperature
  and humidity by **opening to create more air space and insulation, and then closing to
  decrease insulation**" (p. 81).
- **Wind moving across a surface increases thermal loss** — so protecting the insulating
  layer from air movement increases retention. For us this cuts both ways: in summer we
  *want* the wind on the surface (F3), in winter we do not.

Application ideas (p. 82) include: *"Embed passive or reflective heating elements in the
building facade to prevent snow accumulation or direct winter sunlight to create warm
air."*

### Trees: "sun and shade leaves optimize energy source" (p. 92–94)

- **Intracanopy plasticity**: sun leaves are smaller, thicker, with deeper sinuses and more
  stomata per unit area; shade leaves spread mass thinly over a wider area for diffuse
  light. Sun leaves let light through to the interior.
- The design lesson for us is not about light collection but about **differentiating one
  surface by exposure**: the same product need not have the same geometry on a south façade,
  an east façade and a roof. This supports the Section 2 finding that the concept should
  not be restricted to the façade.

## Water chapter (p. 27) — "slow it, sink it, store it"

> "A common pattern of how water flows in nature is **'slow it, sink it, store it'**."

Chapter functions: increase infiltration · slow water · create air flow · purify water ·
**moderate humidity**.

### Lichen: rough surface + hydrophobic compounds (p. 41–44) — maps to F10, F11, F6

- *Lecanora conizaeoides* grows on tree trunks without harming the host. Multi-scale
  roughness keeps water droplets **perched** on top rather than wetting the whole surface;
  channels between the structures are coated with **hydrophobins** so they stay dry and air
  still reaches the algae.
- Design principle (p. 43): **"Breathable, water-shedding surface made of hydrophobic
  compounds combined with multi-layered roughness."**
- Application ideas (p. 44), directly relevant to our KPI 7 and F10:
  - a water-shedding surface treatment that keeps working in low light and rain;
  - a façade skin that is simultaneously waterproof and breathable;
  - **"The way that lichens shed water could be incorporated into structures used to
    capture water, resulting in less adherence and therefore more complete capture and
    fewer opportunities for buildup of algae and biofilms."** — i.e. the same structure
    serves F6 (capture) and F10 (stay clean), which is a multi-functionality argument, and
    it is a **physical** answer to the soiling problem rather than a chemical one.

### Bryophytes: "absorbable, water-holding material moderates humidity" (p. 53–56) — maps to F6–F8

- Mosses have no waxy cuticle, no vascular system and no roots, so tissue **absorbs water
  immediately**. A dense double layer — living green zone over a brown decaying zone —
  combined with a **still air layer that protects against evaporation** holds moisture.
  When they do dry out they desiccate and **resurrect when moisture returns**.
- Design principle (p. 55): "To modify humidity, small, simple structures in a dense,
  double layer absorb and release moisture in a still-air environment."
- Application ideas (p. 56): **"Develop or find a building facade material that can absorb
  water from the air and either hold it for later use or drain it into a reservoir. Use in
  combination with a water-impermeable layer to prevent deeper saturation into more
  sensitive building materials."**

That last one is close to a description of our F6–F7 subsystem, including the caveat about
protecting the substrate — which is also a retrofit constraint for us.

The **resurrection behaviour** is worth a second look for the five-day design case: a
material that survives running dry and recovers when it rains again fails gracefully
instead of failing permanently.

## Life's Principles as the report applies them (p. 60)

Its energy chapter maps the principles like this — a template for our Section 4:

- *Be locally attuned and responsive* — "design heating and cooling systems to be
  responsive to ambient conditions and needs of people within a space"
- *Adapt to changing conditions* — "embody resilience through variation, redundancy and
  decentralization: decentralize solar collectors and vary their design, placement and type
  based on exposure to light and shade"
- *Be resource-efficient* — "use one design to perform more than one function"
- *Use life-friendly chemistry* — "transport water to cool without using polluting energies"

## What to take into our report

1. The leaf design principle (p. 63) is the closest published statement of the mechanism our
   design question asks for. Cite it as an abstraction, not as a source for physics.
2. The lichen strategy (p. 41–44) answers F10 **physically**, which is what the
   life-friendly-chemistry constraint pushes us towards anyway.
3. The bryophyte strategy (p. 53–56) is the water-path model, including the still-air layer
   and the resurrection behaviour.
4. The 8 °C urban-vs-forest figure (p. 62) is a useful framing sentence, but for numbers we
   stay with the City of Zurich data — this report is US-oriented and not a measurement
   source for us.
