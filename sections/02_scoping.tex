\section{Challenge \& Scoping}

\subsection{Problem definition and design question}
\label{sec:designquestion}

\textbf{Given challenge (Challenge~1).} \emph{Cities are facing increasing heat
stress due to climate change. Develop bioinspired strategies to reduce urban
heat through building materials, façade design, or urban planning.}

The challenge as given spans three different scales --- material, building
component and city planning. Treating all three would make the project broader
rather than more focused, which the systems view in
Section~\ref{sec:systemview} is explicitly meant to prevent. We therefore
narrow the challenge to the scale at which our team can actually design,
calculate and test something within the available time: the \emph{outer
surface of an existing building envelope}.

A second reason for this cut is physical. The urban heat island is driven by
the surface energy balance: solar radiation absorbed by opaque urban surfaces
during the day is stored and re-emitted as sensible heat, while long-wave loss
to the sky is reduced by the limited sky view factor of the street canyon
\parencite{oke1982}. The building envelope is one of the largest of these
surfaces and, unlike streets or trees, it is directly accessible to a design
intervention by a single building owner.

\medskip
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{0.97\textwidth}{%
\textbf{Design question (solution-neutral):}\\[2mm]
\emph{How might we keep the outer envelope of existing urban buildings --- and
the air and surfaces next to it --- cool during summer heat waves, using only
passive means and locally available water?}}}

\medskip
The question was deliberately formulated \emph{after} the context, systems and
function analysis, not before. It names no solution (no coating, no greening,
no ventilation principle), states the operating context (existing buildings,
summer heat waves), and fixes the two hard constraints that follow from
Section~\ref{sec:context} (no auxiliary energy, no potable water). Following
the guidance on scoping questions, it sits between \enquote{How might we cool
the city?} (too broad --- no defined intervention point) and \enquote{How might
we design a reflective roof paint?} (too narrow --- presupposes the solution).

\subsection{Context and constraints}
\label{sec:context}

\paragraph{Reference city: Zurich.} All context values, benchmarks and
engineering checks in this report refer to the City of Zurich
(\ang{47.38}\,N, \ang{8.54}\,E, approximately \qty{400}{\metre} above sea
level on the valley floor of the Limmat). Fixing one reference city rather than
designing for \enquote{a city} is what makes the constraints in
Table~\ref{tab:constraints} and the KPI targets in Table~\ref{tab:kpi}
checkable instead of generic. Zurich was chosen for four reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The city has an adopted municipal heat-mitigation strategy, the
        \emph{Fachplanung Hitzeminderung} \parencite{stadtzuerich2020}, with an
        implementation agenda \parencite{stadtzuerich_agenda}, a guidance
        leaflet for building owners \parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt} and three
        thematic maps \parencite{stadtzuerich_teilplaene}. The project therefore
        has a real client, a documented baseline and published target values
        instead of invented ones. Section~\ref{sec:stateofart} shows what this
        means for our design space.
  \item The city publishes an open climate analysis and open geodata
        \parencite{stadtzuerich_opendata}, so the reference site can be
        selected from measured heat-load data instead of intuition.
  \item MeteoSwiss operates long-term stations in and around the city
        \parencite{meteoswiss}, which supplies the boundary conditions
        ($G$, $T_a$, wind speed, cloud cover) for the energy balance in
        Section~3.2.
  \item Its building stock and morphology are representative of the Swiss
        Mittelland: dense perimeter-block development, narrow street canyons, a
        high sealed fraction and a large share of buildings predating modern
        thermal standards. Results can therefore be transferred to comparable
        Swiss cities.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Reference site and users.} We adopt the city's own model area
\emph{Modellierungsgebiet 03 --- Geschlossene Randbebauung} (closed perimeter
block) as the reference site \parencite[148--151]{stadtzuerich2020}. It consists
of two outward-closed blocks of five storeys plus a roof structure enclosing
private courtyards, in a dense district close to the centre; the street space is
sealed from façade to façade, parking runs along the street and only isolated
trees provide shade. The city's own climatic assessment of this structural type
is that the reduced air exchange and the lack of cold-air supply make it
overheat comparatively quickly.

Choosing this site rather than an arbitrary building has a concrete methodical
advantage: the Fachplanung already publishes modelled baseline and
climate-optimised results for exactly this area, so our concept can be compared
against a documented reference instead of against our own assumptions.

The system users are the residents of the upper floors, who carry the strongest
heat load and usually have no mechanical cooling; the pedestrians in the street
canyon, who receive radiation from the façade at eye level; and the building
owner, who decides on and pays for the retrofit.

\paragraph{When.} The design must perform in two distinct operating windows,
which the systems view made explicit:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Day window (approx.\ 11:00--18:00, clear sky):} clear-sky
        global irradiance on a horizontal surface of the order of
        \qty{900}{\watt\per\square\metre} to
        \qty{950}{\watt\per\square\metre} around solar noon in midsummer, with
        air temperatures reaching roughly \qtyrange{33}{36}{\celsius} during a
        heat wave. Dominant problem: absorbed short-wave radiation.
        A sealed, sun-exposed surface in the city can exceed
        \qty{60}{\celsius} in summer and drive up the air temperature in its
        immediate surroundings \parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt}.
        \todo{replace the irradiance and air-temperature figures with measured
        values from MeteoSwiss for the Zurich stations --- the irradiance above
        is a clear-sky estimate, not a measurement}
  \item \textbf{Night window (approx.\ 22:00--06:00):} the stored heat is
        released again, producing tropical nights ($T_\mathrm{min} \geq
        \qty{20}{\celsius}$). Dominant problem: the \emph{delayed} release of
        stored heat, and a reduced sky view factor in the street canyon that
        limits radiative loss. \textbf{For Zurich the night is the larger half
        of the problem:} the city states an urban heat island effect of
        \qtyrange{1}{2}{\kelvin} by day but up to \qty{7}{\kelvin} at night in
        the centre compared with the surrounding area
        \parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt}. A concept that only performs during
        the day therefore addresses the smaller half.

        itself a result worth reporting}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Where on the envelope? A consequence of Zurich's latitude.}
Fixing the reference city also fixes the solar geometry, and that geometry
already narrows the design space before any biology is considered. At
\ang{47.38}\,N the solar altitude at noon on 21 June is

\begin{equation}
h = 90^{\circ} - 47.38^{\circ} + 23.44^{\circ} \approx 66^{\circ} .
\label{eq:solaraltitude}
\end{equation}

\noindent The direct beam therefore strikes a horizontal roof at an incidence
factor of $\sin h \approx 0.91$, but a vertical south-facing façade at only
$\cos h \approx 0.41$. In midsummer --- exactly when the concept has to work
--- the flat roof receives well over twice the direct irradiance of a south
façade at noon, and the roof also has a much larger sky view factor for
night-time long-wave emission (F2). \todo{Confirm with daily irradiation totals
per surface orientation from the national roof-irradiance dataset
\parencite{sonnendach}; then decide and justify whether the concept targets the
roof, the façade or both. Note that east and west façades peak in the morning
and late afternoon rather than at noon, so a façade concept has a different
load profile, not simply a smaller one.}

\subsection{State of the art: what the City of Zurich already does}
\label{sec:stateofart}

Scoping is incomplete without knowing what the client already does. The
Fachplanung Hitzeminderung defines thirteen courses of action
(\emph{Handlungsansätze}, HA~01--HA~13). Four of them act on the building
envelope and therefore fall inside our project boundary: HA~09 green roofs,
HA~10 green façades, HA~11 high-albedo façade and roof materials, and HA~12
shading of the outdoor space close to the building
\parencite[120--131]{stadtzuerich2020}. HA~11 is the direct incumbent of our
design question, and the city already recommends it to building owners
\parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt}.

\paragraph{Baseline values this fixes for us.} The city's model uses a solar
reflectance of $0.2$ for asphalt and $0.3$ for un-greened façades, and
classifies $\leq 0.30$ as low and $\geq 0.80$ as high albedo. Reported material
values are slate $0.1$, roof tiles $0.1$--$0.35$, natural stone $0.2$--$0.35$
and a white façade $0.5$--$0.9$ \parencite[114, 128--129]{stadtzuerich2020}.
Our reference case and the target of KPI~1 in Table~\ref{tab:kpi} are taken
directly from these figures, so the comparison is made on the client's own
terms rather than on ours.

\paragraph{Constraints the city already identified for HA 11.} Glare and the
resulting safety concerns; reduced yield of façade-integrated photovoltaics;
higher construction and refurbishment cost; susceptibility to soiling and the
cleaning cost that follows; and heritage protection requirements
\parencite[128]{stadtzuerich2020}. These are carried into
Table~\ref{tab:constraints} as sourced constraints.

\paragraph{The gap --- and the design opportunity.} Table~\ref{tab:incumbent}
contains the central finding for this project. The incumbent measure works
during the day but is close to ineffective at night: \qty{-0.1}{\kelvin}
median air temperature, against an urban heat island that reaches
\qty{7}{\kelvin} at night. A bright surface reduces what is absorbed, but it
provides no additional path for releasing heat that has already been stored in
the construction, and it does nothing that would help once the sun is down.

This defines the space in which a bio-inspired concept can be more than a
repetition of existing practice. It must do at least one of the following:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item beat HA~11 on its own daytime metric (PET at pedestrian level), or
  \item deliver a night-time effect that HA~11 does not --- which is what the
        biologized questions on emitting to the night sky (F2) and on storing,
        buffering and metering water (F4, F6--F8) are aimed at, or
  \item resolve one of the constraints the city itself names for HA~11 ---
        above all glare and soiling, which are exactly the points where the
        biological models are strong: structural rather than specular
        whiteness, and self-cleaning surfaces.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the systems view added.}
\emph{The systems view changed our project scope because} it showed that the
envelope does not only exchange heat with the building interior, but also with
two systems we had not considered: the pedestrian level of the street canyon
(the façade radiates onto people) and the night sky (the only heat sink that
is available without energy input). This added one function
(\emph{emit long-wave radiation to the sky}), one constraint (the concept must
also perform at night, not just at the daytime peak) and one KPI
(net radiative cooling power at night, KPI~5 in Table~\ref{tab:kpi}). The
comparison with the parallel system \enquote{urban vegetation} also sharpened a
constraint: vegetation achieves its cooling through evapotranspiration and
therefore needs a continuous water supply, which in a heat wave is exactly the
resource that is scarce. This is the origin of the hard constraint
\enquote{no potable water}.

\subsection{Biologized questions}
\label{sec:biologized}

The functions are translated into questions to nature, which form the search
strategy for Section~3.1:

\begin{itemize}
  \item How does nature reflect intense solar radiation without pigments that
        bleach --- and how does it stay white or bright over a lifetime?
  \item How does nature release excess heat to the night sky?
  \item How does nature dissipate heat where water is scarce?
  \item How does nature capture, store and meter out small amounts of water?
  \item How does nature buffer a daily temperature extreme instead of
        resisting it?
  \item How does nature keep a functional surface clean without cleaning it?
  \item How does nature repair a surface that is damaged by weathering?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{One quantity deliberately not a KPI.} The heat conducted through the
envelope into the building is reported in Section~3.2 as a secondary result,
because it follows from the same energy balance at no extra cost and matters to
the building owner. It is not a selection KPI: indoor comfort and building
services lie outside the project boundary (Table~\ref{tab:inout}), and mixing
an out-of-boundary benefit into the concept comparison would distort it.

\paragraph{Note on the targets.} The target values above are the team's
\emph{aspirational} benchmarks set during scoping. They are hypotheses, not
results. Every one of them is checked in Section~3.2 and either confirmed,
revised or explicitly declared unverified. \todo{before hand-in: verify each
cited benchmark value against the primary source --- the rubric penalises
unchecked claims}

\paragraph{Important distinction.} KPI~3 measures a \emph{surface} temperature,
not the air temperature of the city. A high-reflectance surface can easily be
\qty{20}{\kelvin} cooler than a dark one, while the effect on the air
temperature of the district is smaller by roughly two orders of magnitude
\parencite{santamouris2014,akbari2009}. Conflating the two is the most common
error in urban-heat-island projects and would invalidate our evaluation, so
both quantities are reported separately throughout this report.

\subsection{Relevant Life's Principles}
\label{sec:lifesprinciples}

The Life's Principles that are most relevant to this specific challenge, and
that are used again as a sustainability benchmark in Section~4:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Use low energy processes} --- the entire cooling function must
        be passive; this is the hard energy constraint.
  \item \textbf{Be locally attuned and responsive} --- the concept must respond
        to the local diurnal cycle and the local water availability, not to an
        average annual climate.
  \item \textbf{Be resource efficient (material and energy)} --- fit form to
        function, low added mass per square metre (see also KPI~7).
  \item \textbf{Adapt to changing conditions} --- performance is needed in a
        summer heat wave, but the same surface must not harm the winter
        energy balance.
  \item \textbf{Maintain integrity through self-renewal} --- the optical
        function degrades through soiling; a self-cleaning or self-renewing
        surface preserves the KPI over the service life.
  \item \textbf{Use life-friendly chemistry} --- relevant when selecting
        pigments, binders and biocides for the outer layer.
\end{itemize}
