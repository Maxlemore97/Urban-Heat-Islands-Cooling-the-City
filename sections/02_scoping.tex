\section{Challenge \& Scoping}

\subsection{Problem definition and design question}
\label{sec:designquestion}

\textbf{Given challenge (Challenge~1).} \emph{Cities are facing increasing heat
stress due to climate change. Develop bioinspired strategies to reduce urban
heat through building materials, façade design, or urban planning.}

The challenge as given spans three scales --- material, building component and
urban planning. Working on all three would make the project larger rather than
more focused, which the systems view is explicitly meant to prevent. The design
question is the instrument that makes this cut.

\subsubsection{Candidate questions considered}

Four candidates were formulated in the team and assessed against the criterion
that a design question must be neither too broad nor too narrow: open enough to
admit different solutions, but specific enough to say what is to be reduced.

  & \emph{Pre-commits to prevention}, which excludes the removal half of the
    problem --- the half the incumbent measure leaves unsolved. Also unanchored
    in place and time. \\
2 & How could we use the energy or heat build-up positively while lowering
    temperature?
  & \emph{Unanchored.} No location, and \enquote{positively} does not say for
    what. \\
3 & How might we get the heat that Zurich's existing buildings store during the
    day back out of the city before the next morning, without using energy?
  & \textbf{Well formed and highly testable}, but it fixes the mechanism (night

    work. Its self-regulating intent is carried forward as a design decision. \\
4 & How can we prevent heat waves causing heat islands?
  & \emph{Factually wrong.} Heat waves do not cause heat islands. The urban heat

    and exists on ordinary summer days; a heat wave makes it dangerous, it does
    not create it \parencite{oke1982}. \\

\end{tabularx}

\subsubsection{Selected design question}

\medskip
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{0.97\textwidth}{%
\textbf{Design question (solution-neutral):}\\[2mm]
\emph{How might we reduce the urban heat island effect in cities like Zurich?}}}

\medskip
The question is stated at the level of the \emph{effect}, not of the
intervention. It names no solution --- no coating, no greening, no geometry, no
water --- and, unlike candidate~1, it does not pre-commit to \emph{preventing}
heat build-up, which would exclude the removal half of the problem. That
exclusion would matter: the city's own modelling puts the effect of a bright
façade at \qty{-1.0}{\kelvin} by day but only \qty{-0.1}{\kelvin} at night
\parencite[Tab.~11, 128]{stadtzuerich2020}, against a heat island that reaches
\qty{7}{\kelvin} after dark \parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt}. The night is the
larger half of the problem and the half the incumbent measure does not address.

What the question deliberately does not do is narrow the project; that is the
job of the rest of this section, and each step is argued rather than assumed.
Section~\ref{sec:context} fixes the reference city, the reference site and the
place the effect must appear, which makes the \textbf{façade} the system of
interest; Section~\ref{sec:systemview} fixes what the design may touch;
Section~\ref{sec:functions} states what it must do; and the criteria in
Section~\ref{sec:kpi} fix what \enquote{reduce} has to mean in numbers.

One property is carried forward from candidate~3 and is not visible in the
wording of the question, so it is stated here explicitly: the response must be
\textbf{self-regulating}, its cooling power rising with the heat load instead of
being fixed. This is a decision of the team rather than a demand of the
question, and it is the decision that separates the project from the incumbent
measure. A bright surface has a fixed optical response: it reflects the same
fraction of radiation at \qty{20}{\celsius} as at \qty{40}{\celsius}, and it
does nothing at all once the sun has set. A self-regulating surface behaves like
a biological one --- a leaf's response is driven by the same solar energy that
heats the leaf, so the cooling effort scales with the load, without a
controller, a sensor or a pump. It becomes function~F7 and is measured by the
first performance criterion.

That decision also resolves the team's initial difficulty of having two systems
of interest (\enquote{reduce heat} and \enquote{prevent heat}). These are not
two systems. They are two functions of one system: prevention acts during the
day window, removal during the night window, and the self-regulating link
connects them.

\subsection{Context and constraints}
\label{sec:context}

\paragraph{Reference city: Zurich.} All context values, benchmarks and
engineering checks in this report refer to the City of Zurich. Fixing one
reference city is what makes the constraints and the criteria checkable instead
of generic. Zurich was chosen for four reasons: it has an adopted municipal
heat-mitigation strategy, the \emph{Fachplanung Hitzeminderung}
\parencite{stadtzuerich2020}, with an implementation agenda
\parencite{stadtzuerich_agenda}, a guidance leaflet for building owners
\parencite{stadtzuerich_merkblatt} and three thematic maps
\parencite{stadtzuerich_teilplaene}, so the project has a real client, a
documented baseline and published target values instead of invented ones; it
publishes an open climate analysis and open geodata
\parencite{stadtzuerich_opendata}, so the reference site can be selected from
measured heat-load data rather than intuition; MeteoSwiss operates long-term
stations in and around the city \parencite{meteoswiss}, which supplies the
boundary conditions ($G$, $T_a$, wind speed, humidity) for the energy balance in
Section~3.2; and its building stock and morphology are representative of the
Swiss Mittelland, so results transfer to comparable Swiss cities.

\paragraph{Reference site and users.} We adopt the city's own model area
\emph{Modellierungsgebiet 03 --- Geschlossene Randbebauung} (closed perimeter
block) as the reference site \parencite[148--151]{stadtzuerich2020}. It consists
of two outward-closed blocks of five storeys plus a roof structure enclosing
private courtyards, in a dense district close to the centre; the street space is
sealed from façade to façade and only isolated trees provide shade. The city's
own assessment is that the reduced air exchange and the lack of cold-air supply
make this structural type overheat comparatively quickly. Choosing this site has
a methodical advantage: the Fachplanung already publishes modelled baseline and
climate-optimised results for exactly this area, so our concept can be compared
against a documented reference rather than against our own assumptions.

the specific building you pick inside this area.}

The users are the residents of the upper floors, who carry the strongest heat
load and usually have no mechanical cooling; the pedestrians in the street
canyon, who receive radiation from the façade at eye level; the building owner,
who decides on and pays for the retrofit; and the trades who install and later
maintain the system.

\paragraph{The design case.} The design case is not a single hot afternoon but a
\textbf{multi-day heat wave}: five consecutive hot days without rain. Within
each day, three windows matter and they follow one another without a gap,
because the surface never stops exchanging heat --- the \emph{load} window
around solar noon, when the energy that has to be dealt with all night enters;
the \emph{evening} from 18:00, when people are outdoors and west façades reach
their highest incidence angle; and the \emph{night} to 06:00, when the store
must be discharged before the next day begins. A concept that works on day one
and is saturated by day three has not solved the problem.

\subsection{System view and project boundary}
\label{sec:systemview}

% comment 0: intro reworked -- it now says what the two devices are FOR
A system view is a deliberate change of altitude. Before deciding what to build,
we ask what the object sits inside, what sits inside it, and what else solves a
comparable problem next to it. The purpose is not tidiness: the level
\emph{above} shows which effects we can influence and which we merely inherit,
the level \emph{below} shows where a design decision actually lands, and the
systems \emph{beside} show which resources and constraints already exist. The
boundary drawn afterwards then does the opposite job --- it keeps the project
finishable by naming what we will not touch. Table~\ref{tab:hierarchy} lists the
levels and Figure~\ref{fig:systemview} draws them with the boundary in place.

\noindent Read downwards, the table says the following. The \textbf{super-system}
is the city: its microclimate, the geometry of the street canyon and the
radiation balance between the two are what produce the heat island, and they are
the conditions our design meets rather than quantities it can set. The
\textbf{system of interest} is deliberately one layer and not a building --- the
outer skin is the surface where the city's radiation actually arrives and where
the building's stored heat is actually released, so it is the thinnest object
that still contains the whole problem. The \textbf{parallel systems} are the
three that already act on the same street: the pavement, treated by the same
client with the same high-albedo logic, which sets a benchmark our envelope has
to beat; the power grid and building energy system, which is the route a
conventional answer would take and which the energy constraint closes to us; and
the residents, who fix \emph{where} the effect has to appear --- at head height
in the canyon, not on the roof membrane. The \textbf{subsystems} are what a
concept may actually vary, and they are components rather than people: the
optical outer surface, the layer behind it that stores and insulates, the
materials both are made of, and the fixing to the wall.

\subsection{Functions and functional diagram}
\label{sec:functions}

Functions are what the design must \emph{do}, written as verbs and kept free of
any solution. They are stated at the scale of the designed object, not at the
scale of the city: the design regulates heat at the surface it occupies, and the
city-scale effect is the \emph{goal}, not the function --- in the same way that
the function of polar bear fur is to insulate, not to make the Arctic habitable.

% comment 2: what a functional diagram actually is
\paragraph{What the functional diagram shows.} A functional diagram is the
function list drawn as a flow picture. It contains only \emph{functions} and the
\emph{flows} between them --- energy, material and information --- and
deliberately no components and no materials, so that it stays solution-neutral:
the same diagram must remain true whichever concept is later chosen. Reading
Figure~\ref{fig:funcdiagram} left to right, solar radiation arrives and is
either turned away (F2, F3) or absorbed and buffered (F6); what is stored leaves
again by radiation to the sky (F4) or by convection into the canyon air (F5);
and whatever neither path removes is the residual flow into the building, which
the design exists to minimise. The dashed orange arrow is F7 and is the only
loop in the picture: the surface temperature itself feeds back into how strongly
the removal functions act.

\paragraph{Why F7 is physically plausible.} Every removal path already depends
on surface temperature, but not equally strongly. Differentiated with respect to
$T_s$, the self-regulation gradients are, in
\unit{\watt\per\square\metre\per\kelvin}, radiative
$4\varepsilon\sigma T_s^{3} \approx 6$ and convective $h_c \approx 10$. The
convective path is the steeper of the two, and it is also the one a surface can
influence through geometry --- which is why boundary-layer texture and
self-shading relief, rather than a material switch alone, are the candidates
carried into Section~3.2. \todo{Evaluate both derivatives with your own
assumptions and state the ranking; this is the check that decides which
mechanism carries F7.}

\subsection{Biologized questions}
\label{sec:biologized}

% comment 5: each question is now tied to the function it searches for
The functions are translated into questions to nature, which form the search
strategy for Section~3.1. Each question names the function it searches for, so
that the biology found in Section~3.1 can be traced back to a requirement rather
than collected for its own interest. The challenge brief also asks directly what
can be learned from desert plants and animals about passive cooling, and the
answer shapes where we looked: the organisms most challenged by our problem, and
unfazed by it, live where solar load is extreme and water is scarce.

\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item \textbf{F1} --- How does nature use the heat it receives to get rid of
        that same heat?
  \item \textbf{F2} --- How does nature reflect intense solar radiation without
        pigments that bleach, and without a mirror finish?
  \item \textbf{F3} --- How does nature shade a surface, even in the desert?
  \item \textbf{F4} --- How does nature release excess heat to the night sky?
  \item \textbf{F5} --- How does nature move heat into the air around it?
  \item \textbf{F6} --- How does nature buffer a daily temperature extreme
        instead of resisting it?
  \item \textbf{F7} --- How does nature make a response stronger when the load is
        stronger, without a controller?
  \item \textbf{F8} --- How does nature keep a functional surface clean without
        cleaning it?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Performance criteria and KPIs}
\label{sec:kpi}

% comment 6: prose with one paragraph per criterion, each tied to a Life's Principle
The criteria are derived from the functions, interfaces and constraints
identified above, not invented independently of them. They are used twice: to
guide the engineering checks in Section~3.2 and to compare the concepts in
Section~3.3. The reference case throughout is an existing Zurich façade with a
solar reflectance of about $0.3$, the value the city uses for un-greened façades
\parencite[114]{stadtzuerich2020}. Each criterion also names the Life's
Principle it carries, so that the principles are scored with the numbers rather
than listed beside them; Section~\ref{sec:sustainability} then assesses how well
the concept achieves them and where it falls short.

\paragraph{1 --- Self-regulation gradient (F7).} The rise in cooling power per
kelvin of surface temperature, $\partial P_\mathrm{cool}/\partial T_s$, in
\unit{\watt\per\square\metre\per\kelvin}. It must be clearly positive and larger
than for a fixed high-albedo surface. This is the defining criterion of the
project: it is the quantity that separates a self-regulating envelope from a
bright one, and no other criterion can substitute for it. It carries \emph{be
locally attuned and responsive} --- the signal, the antenna that detects it and
the response must match \parencite{baumeister2014}. \todo{Set a numeric target
once the derivative check in Section~3.2 is done.}

\paragraph{2 --- Solar reflectance (F2).} Solar reflectance
$\alpha_\mathrm{sol}$ over \qtyrange{300}{2500}{\nano\metre}, dimensionless.
Target $\geq 0.80$, the threshold the city itself defines for \enquote{high
albedo} \parencite[128]{stadtzuerich2020}, against a baseline of $0.3$.
Ultra-white laboratory paints reach about $0.98$ \parencite{li2021}, and we
deliberately do not aim there: the umbrella principle \emph{create conditions
conducive to life} asks for a design \emph{optimised rather than maximised}, and
past the city's threshold further reflectance is bought with glare, lost winter
gains and reduced photovoltaic yield.

\paragraph{3 --- Peak surface temperature reduction (F1).} The drop in peak
surface temperature $\Delta T_s$ against the reference façade, clear sky, 14:00,
in kelvin. Target $\geq \qty{15}{\kelvin}$. \todo{Confirm by calculation or
measurement before claiming it.}

\paragraph{4 --- Effect on people (F1).} The reduction in physiologically
equivalent temperature at \qty{2}{\metre} above ground at 14:00, in kelvin ---
the metric the client itself uses. The binding benchmark is the
\qty{-1.0}{\kelvin} median that the city models for a bright \emph{façade}; the
\qty{-1.2}{\kelvin} it models for a bright roof is context, not our target,
because the system of interest is the façade
\parencite[Tab.~11, 128]{stadtzuerich2020}. Criteria 3 and 4 must not be
confused: a high-reflectance surface can be \qty{20}{\kelvin} cooler than a dark
one while the effect on district air temperature is smaller by roughly two
orders of magnitude \parencite{santamouris2014,akbari2009}. Conflating them is
the most common error in urban-heat-island projects, so they are reported
separately throughout.

\paragraph{5 --- Night-time removal (F4, F6).} Net cooling power at 02:00 under
a clear sky, in \unit{\watt\per\square\metre}, together with the heat discharged
across the evening and night windows, 18:00--06:00, in
\unit{\watt\hour\per\square\metre}. Target: at least
\qty{40}{\watt\per\square\metre} instantaneous, the order of magnitude
demonstrated for passive radiative cooling surfaces
\parencite{raman2014,zhai2017}, and a night-time air temperature effect better
than the \qty{-0.1}{\kelvin} of the incumbent
\parencite[Tab.~11, 128]{stadtzuerich2020}. A second condition applies across
the five-day design case rather than within a day: the surface temperature and
the state of charge of the store at 06:00 must not drift upwards from one day to
the next. A concept that cools well on the first day and saturates by the third
fails the design case even if every single-day number is met. This criterion
carries \emph{adapt to changing conditions}, sub-principle \emph{adapt to
temporal change}.

\paragraph{6 --- Durability of the function (F8).} Retention of the cooling
function after soiling and weathering, in per cent of the initial value. Target
$\geq \qty{90}{\percent}$ after three years. Soiling is the weakness the city
names for high-albedo envelopes \parencite[128]{stadtzuerich2020}, and aged cool
roofs commonly lose a significant fraction of their initial reflectance
\parencite{santamouris2014}. It carries \emph{maintain integrity through
self-renewal}, and it is the criterion that rules a chemical answer to soiling
out: \emph{use life-friendly chemistry} forbids a biocide whose breakdown
products leave the façade for the street below.
