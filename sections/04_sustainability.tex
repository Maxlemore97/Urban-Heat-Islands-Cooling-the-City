\section{Sustainability \& Life's Principles}
\label{sec:sustainability}

\subsection{What this section assesses, and what it does not claim}

Section~\ref{sec:kpi} named a Life's Principle beside each performance criterion.
Naming them is not applying them, and the difference is the whole content of
this section: each principle below is stated as a \emph{claim}, followed by the
evidence for it and by the point at which the concept falls short of it. A
principle with no shortfall named is a principle that has not been assessed.

Two limits are stated at the outset rather than buried. First, the concept
assessed here is Concept~V2 of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} --- a ribbed
new-build façade carrying a closed, pumpless water loop, with a sprayed
millimetre texture on the façade panels and the roof plate. It is a described
concept, not a dimensioned design, and the assessment can be no more specific
than that. Second, no life cycle assessment was
carried out. Where embodied emissions are discussed they are discussed as a
\emph{criterion with a decision rule}, not as a computed result, and the words
\enquote{sustainable} and \enquote{resource efficient} are avoided as
descriptions of the concept because on the present evidence neither could be
defended.

\subsection{Eco-efficient or eco-effective?}

Cradle to Cradle draws a distinction that this project has to face directly
\parencite{braungart2002}. An \emph{eco-efficient} design is less bad: it does
the same thing while emitting less. An \emph{eco-effective} design is actively
good: its outputs are inputs somewhere else. The standard illustration is a
brake pad --- one version emits fewer particles but still emits thousands of
tonnes, the other is made of a material that can safely re-enter a biological
cycle. Nature, the argument runs, is not efficient at all: a cherry tree
produces blossom wastefully, and is nonetheless effective because every part of
that waste is a nutrient.

Measured against that distinction, Concept~V2 is \textbf{eco-efficient and no
more}. A façade that absorbs less, moves what it does absorb to a better surface
and gives it away without energy is a less bad façade; it produces no nutrient
for anything.

One thing is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to overclaim. The loop
is \emph{closed}: no water is consumed, none evaporates, and nothing is drawn
from the municipal supply. Against an evaporative façade --- the obvious alternative, the direction an
earlier version of this scoping carried, and one already built as
\emph{HydroCanopy} \parencite{asknature_hydrocanopy} --- that
is a genuine advantage under the water constraint of
Table~\ref{tab:constraints}, and it is the honest way to state it: V2 avoids a
resource cost rather than creating a resource benefit. Avoiding a cost is
efficiency. It is not effectiveness.

An eco-effective version of this concept would have to make its output the input
of something else. Two routes exist and both were placed outside the boundary in
Section~\ref{sec:systemview}: recovering the collected heat for domestic hot
water, which the boundary excludes as \enquote{reuse of the stored energy}
because it turns the project into a building-services design; and harvesting the
rainwater that falls on the same roof plate, which would reconnect the discarded
stormwater the parallel-system comparison identified. Neither is pursued here,
and the honest summary is that the project remains at the efficient end by a
boundary decision rather than by a physical limit. That is the first thing a
follow-on project should revisit.

\subsection{The material perspective: which layer belongs in which cycle}

Cradle to Cradle divides materials into \emph{biological nutrients}, which are
consumed and must return safely to the environment, and \emph{technical
nutrients}, which are used without being consumed and are recovered
\parencite{braungart2002}. The rule that assigns a material to one or the other
is about wear:

\begin{quote}
Anything that is consumed --- worn out --- has to come from the biological
cycle. Anything that is merely used, without wear, can come from the technical
nutrient cycle.
\end{quote}

Applied layer by layer, this decides the material strategy of the build-up
before any material has been chosen, and it produces one uncomfortable result.

\paragraph{The uncomfortable result.} The layer we would most readily engineer
with chemistry --- the sprayed texture, where the convective performance lives
--- is precisely the layer that must be biologically benign, because it is the
one designed to wear away, and it wears into the place we are trying to
improve. That inverts the usual
order of a coating specification, in which durability and optical performance
are chosen first and the environmental profile is checked afterwards.

Items 6 and 7 are the honest weaknesses here, and they share a cause: both need
somebody to own the material after its first life, which needs a business model
--- rental, service or deposit --- rather than a sale. In a sales model ownership
transfers, so the value created by durable, recoverable design accrues to the
recycler, the user and the maintenance trade rather than to whoever paid for it.
That split incentive is named as the principal obstacle to circular construction
\parencite{emf_denmark}, and this project has it in an acute form: the owner
pays, the tenant gains comfort, the street gains the cooling, and nobody owns the
outcome.

\subsection{Environmental benefits, limitations and trade-offs}

\paragraph{Summer against winter.} A high solar reflectance reduces summer heat
gain and also reduces useful passive solar gains in winter. The constraint in
Table~\ref{tab:constraints} requires that heating demand must not appreciably
rise. \todo{Estimate both halves for the Swiss climate and give the net annual
balance. Until that number exists this is a known unquantified risk, not a
solved trade-off.}

\paragraph{Local against global.} The concept moves heat out of the street
canyon. Whether it \emph{reduces} the total energy in the system or merely
\emph{redistributes} it is a different question, and the honest answer differs
by mechanism, and V2 uses both. The roof plate radiates to the sky, which sends
energy out of the system and is a genuine removal. The sprayed texture works by
convection, which relocates heat into the canyon air rather than removing it ---
and on the façade that air is at head height. Criterion~4 already penalises this:
it is why Concept~B alone scored 2 there. \todo{State which of these the final concept relies on, and do not claim
a reduction where the mechanism only redistributes.}

\paragraph{Frost protection against life-friendly chemistry.} This is the
trade-off V2 created and it has no comfortable answer. The loop must survive to
about \qty{-10}{\celsius} without auxiliary energy. Glycol is the standard
solution and would put a mobile additive inside a pipe running the full height of
an occupied façade, where the failure mode is a leak into the wall or the ground.
Draining for winter avoids the substance but also stops the system working in the
shoulder seasons and adds an operation somebody must remember. A third route ---
sizing and routing the loop so that it is self-draining by gravity --- keeps the
chemistry clean and is the one worth checking first, because it is the only
option that does not trade a principle for a season.
\todo{Establish which of the three is feasible, and if an additive is
unavoidable, name it and check it as Section~\ref{sec:leaching} requires.}

\subsection{Circular does not mean sustainable}
\label{sec:leaching}

Keeping a material in circulation is not by itself an environmental benefit. The
standard counter-example is an additive that is harmless in its first
application, in a laptop housing, and hazardous in its second after recycling
into a children's toy: the substance did not change, the exposure pathway did.

Our project has the same failure mode one step earlier, and it is the reason the
life-friendly-chemistry principle is not decorative here. A biocide or a
stabiliser that is entirely acceptable \emph{in} a façade coating becomes a
different question once it leaves that coating --- and in V2 the sprayed texture
is not merely worn, it is \emph{designed} to be consumed and renewed, so
everything in it reaches the street eventually and by intention rather than by
failure. What it wears into is the street canyon
directly below --- the same pedestrian-level space Section~1 names as the place
the whole project exists to improve. The concept would be depositing its own
emissions exactly where its beneficiaries stand.

Two consequences follow. The self-cleaning function must be structural rather
than chemical --- which the lichen strategy of Section~\ref{sec:discovering}
shows is physically possible and the commercial lotus-effect coating cited in
Section~\ref{sec:designquestion} cites as prior art shows is manufacturable. And any substance that does
remain in the specification must be checked rather than assumed.

\subsection{Did sustainability considerations change the design?}

The template asks this directly, and it is the question a sustainability section
most easily fails, because the honest answer is often no. Here it is yes, in
three documented instances, each traceable to a decision recorded in
Table~\ref{tab:decisionlog} in Appendix~B.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The reflectance target was lowered from what is technically
        possible.} Ultra-white coatings reach a solar reflectance of about
        $0.98$ \parencite{li2021}, and an earlier version of this project treated
        that as the goal. KPI~2 now targets $\geq0.80$, the city's own threshold,
        because the principle of optimising rather than maximising makes the
        further reflectance a cost in glare, winter gains and photovoltaic yield
        rather than a benefit. The performance target was changed by a
        sustainability argument.
  \item \textbf{The self-cleaning function was constrained to a physical
        mechanism.} The obvious solution to soiling is a biocide in the film. The
        leaching argument of Section~\ref{sec:leaching} removed that option
        before any concept was developed, which is why the lichen strategy
        entered the model table at all.
  \item \textbf{The wearing layer was chosen to be the renewable one.} In
        Concept~A the part that degrades sits behind the panel and cannot be
        reached; in V2 it is the sprayed texture on the outside. That was
        selected on a durability and maintainability argument
        (Table~\ref{tab:c2c}, items~4 and~5) rather than on cooling
        performance, and it is why the hybrid was preferred to A alone.
  \item \textbf{Cost and embodied emissions were reclassified.} They were
        originally an eighth KPI. Recognising that a KPI must follow from a
        function moved them into the constraints of
        Table~\ref{tab:constraints} --- where a violation disqualifies a concept
        rather than merely scoring against it. The change made the requirement
        stricter, not weaker.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limits of this assessment}

No life cycle assessment was performed, so no statement in this report should be
read as a quantified environmental balance. The embodied emissions of the added
build-up are a constraint with a decision rule and not a measured figure. The
assessment of Life's Principles above is made against a concept direction rather
than a finished design, and \todo{must be repeated against Concept~V2 once
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} selects it}. Finally, two of the eight circular
design principles --- cascade use and return logistics --- are not addressed at
all, and the reason is a boundary decision rather than an analysis: they require
a business model, and this project does not design one.
