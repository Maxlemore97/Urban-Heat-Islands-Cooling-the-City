# Life's Principles — options for Challenge 1 (Urban Heat Islands)

Source: `2 Scoping - Lifes principles 01092026.pdf` (Baumgartner, 31.08.2026) and
`Lifes Principles Checklist.pdf` (Biomimicry 3.8 Resource Handbook).

Two rules from the lecture govern everything below:

> **"Use Life's Principles to guide — not decorate — the design. Choose only relevant
> principles."** (slide 33)

> **"Make them testable. Instead of 'resource efficient', ask: can we achieve the target
> with less material / energy than the reference solution?"** (slide 33)

And slide 29: integrate them *from the beginning* and **define them as KPIs**, then
measure against the checklist as a sustainability benchmark.

Note that slide 33's own worked example is **passive cooling** — "exploit local wind /
temperature differences · minimise operating energy · avoid unnecessary material · adapt
to changing external conditions". That is our challenge almost verbatim, so we are
expected to go further than that list, not repeat it.

## 1. Be locally attuned and responsive — the core principle

Checklist items that apply directly:

- *harnesses freely available energy*
- *leverages existing cycles*
- *detects local feedback loops* · *responds quickly and appropriately to feedback*
- *the signal it sends, the "antennae" it uses to detect the signal, and its response match*
- *is resourceful with opportunities and limitations*
- *materials used are local and abundant*
- *avoids competition by finding a new niche*

### Options

**1a — The load is its own sensor (F9).** The checklist's "signal / antenna / response
match" is exactly the self-regulating link the design question asks for: the surface
temperature is the signal, the physics of the removal path is the antenna, and the cooling
power is the response. No controller, no sensor, no pump. This is the single strongest
mapping we have and it should be stated in Section 2, not only in Section 4.
→ *Testable:* KPI 1, the self-regulation gradient ∂P/∂T<sub>s</sub> in W/m²K, must be
positive and larger than for a fixed high-albedo surface.

**1b — Two freely available sinks, both currently discarded.** "Harnesses freely available
energy" reads for us as *harnesses freely available sinks*: the night sky (F2) and the
rainwater the city currently drains away as fast as possible (F6–F8). The lecture's
biological example is the Namib desert beetle, which is attuned to "the only available
water" — fog. Our equivalent is that during a heat wave it does not rain, so the only
available water is what was captured before it.
→ *Testable:* KPI 5 (net cooling power at 02:00) and KPI 6 (potable water = 0, autonomy
≥ 5 days on harvested rain).

**1c — Photochromic glazing as the human precedent (slide 25).** The lecture's own human
example of this principle is a lens that darkens under UV and reverts without it — a
passive, reversible, load-driven optical change. That is a direct precedent for a
load-responsive *surface* and worth citing when we argue F9 is not exotic.

**1d — "Avoids competition by finding a new niche."** We already have this as a soft
constraint: bright envelopes reduce PV yield, so the concept should state which envelope
areas it claims. Reframed as a principle, the niche is the surface PV does *not* want —
north and east façades, shaded roof strips, parapets.
→ *Testable:* state the claimed area per m² of envelope and show it does not overlap the
PV-viable area from the city's solar cadastre.

**1e — Local and abundant materials.** Open question rather than an answer: is there a
version of the build-up that uses locally abundant mineral material (sand, clay, lime)
rather than an engineered polymer film? Worth one slot in the morphological matrix.

## 2. Be resource efficient (material and energy)

Checklist items that apply:

- *meets its functional needs with minimal outlay of material and energy*
- *a single design meets more than one need*
- *takes the energy path of least resistance*
- *uses shape and information to reduce the use of material and energy* · *the form provides the function*
- *is designed for disassembly, reuse, and reconfiguration*
- *reuses materials or uses recycled materials*
- *taps the power of limits*

### Options

**2a — Use low energy processes: the Trombe wall precedent (slide 16).** The lecture gives
the Trombe wall as the human example — a wall that absorbs solar heat by day and releases
it at night by thermal diffusion, removing the need for mechanical systems. That is our F4
(store thermal energy temporarily) with the sign reversed: the Trombe wall keeps the heat,
we must get rid of it. Naming the difference is a stronger report sentence than naming the
similarity.
→ *Testable:* the hard energy constraint — zero auxiliary electrical energy for the
cooling function.

**2b — One surface, five functions (the duck-preening argument, slide 15).** Our build-up
is asked to reflect (F1), emit (F2), store (F4), handle water (F6–F8), stay clean (F10) and
survive (F11). If a single layer does several of these, that is multi-functionality in the
checklist sense; if each function needs its own layer, we are stacking, not integrating.
→ *Testable:* count functions per physical layer. A concept where one layer carries three
or more functions beats one that needs a layer per function, and this belongs in the
decision matrix as a stated tie-breaker.

**2c — Fit form to function / hollow bones (slide 16).** Material only where it works.
For us: surface geometry that increases the emitting area and provides self-shading (F5)
without adding mass — the opposite of "thicker is better". This connects directly to the
soft constraint on added dead load on an existing envelope.
→ *Testable:* KPI 8, embodied greenhouse gas per m², plus added dead load in kg/m².

**2d — Designed for disassembly.** A retrofit layer on a listed building will be removed
one day. Separability of the layers is already listed as an open point in Section 4.
→ *Testable:* can the build-up be separated into single-material fractions without
solvents? Yes/no, stated per concept.

**2e — "Taps the power of limits."** Our limits are the ones that make the project
interesting: no electricity, no potable water, no structural change, no glare. Say so
explicitly — the constraint table *is* an application of this principle.

## 3. Adapt to changing conditions

Checklist items that apply:

- *adapts to temporal and spatial changes*
- *withstands disturbance while maintaining function*
- *duplicates critical elements* · *functions are distributed and decentralized*
- *incorporates a variety of different forms*
- *maintains integrity through self-renewal* (slide 19)

### Options

**3a — Seasonal switching → the colour-changing coating.** See the dedicated concept
option below; this is the option with the largest consequence for the concept itself.

**3b — Redundancy across removal paths.** "Duplicates critical elements" has a very
concrete reading for the five-day design case: if the evaporative path (F8) runs out of
water on day three, is there still a radiative path (F2) doing something? A concept with
two independent removal mechanisms survives the reset condition; a purely evaporative one
may not.
→ *Testable:* the reset condition — surface temperature and state of charge at 06:00 must
not drift upwards across the five days. Report the trend, not day one.

**3c — Maintain integrity through self-renewal.** Soiling is the weakness the city names
for high-albedo envelopes, and it is our KPI 7. The lecture's human example on slide 19 is
self-healing concrete with resin microcapsules — a precedent for restoring a function
rather than resisting damage.
→ *Testable:* KPI 7, ≥ 90 % of the initial cooling function retained after three years.

**3d — Withstands disturbance while maintaining function.** Frost to −10 °C, freeze–thaw,
driving rain, UV. A water-carrying build-up on a Swiss façade must survive freezing — this
is a genuine risk to any evaporative concept and should be one of the critical engineering
questions in Section 3.2, not a footnote.

## Concept option A — the colour-changing coating (thermochromic envelope)

**The idea.** A coating that is **bright in summer**, so it absorbs little solar energy, and
**dark in winter**, so it absorbs a lot. One surface, two opposite jobs, switched by the
weather instead of by a controller. This is the direct application of *adapt to changing
conditions → incorporate diversity*, and it turns the summer/winter conflict from a
trade-off we accept into a mechanism we design.

### Biological and technical anchors

| Anchor | What it does | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Arctic fox, ptarmigan** | change coat colour with the season | LP lecture, slide 18 — the lecture's own example for this principle |
| ***Encelia farinosa*** | desert shrub; dense leaf pubescence raises reflectance and is **adjusted seasonally** | Ehleringer 1978 — **already in our `references.bib` and already in our model table**. This is the stronger anchor: a plant that changes its own albedo, not just its colour |
| **Photochromic lens** | darkens under UV, reverts without it — passive, reversible, no controller | LP lecture, slide 25, given as the human example of *be locally attuned and responsive* |
| **Adjustable boundary layer** | insulating layer opened and closed seasonally on a roof surface | Genius of Biome p. 70 — the same idea realised geometrically instead of optically |

### Technical routes (for the morphological matrix)

1. **Thermochromic pigment (leuco dye) in a binder** — switches at a set temperature, the
   switching temperature is formulated in. Cheapest route, weakest durability.
2. **Inorganic thermochromic (e.g. vanadium dioxide)** — switches in the infrared at a
   material transition temperature; more durable, more expensive, and its switch is
   mostly in the near-IR rather than the visible.
3. **Non-optical seasonal switch** — a mechanically or hygroscopically actuated layer that
   opens in summer and closes in winter (Genius of Biome p. 70). Keeps the seasonal
   behaviour without relying on dye chemistry.
4. **Manual seasonal limewash** — the traditional low-tech version. Honest baseline to
   compare against: it achieves the same effect for almost nothing, but violates the "little
   maintenance" soft constraint. Worth naming so the comparison is fair.

### Five checks that decide whether this survives

These are the critical engineering questions this concept generates. They belong in
Section 3.2 as they stand.

1. **It switches absorptance, not emittance.** Visible colour governs the short-wave side
   (F1). Thermal emittance in the long-wave band — the path that carries F2 at night — is
   largely independent of visible colour. So this concept improves **prevention**, not
   **removal**: it fixes HA 11's *winter* weakness, and does nothing about HA 11's *night*
   weakness, which is the gap our design question targets. **Treat it as a complement to a
   removal mechanism, not as the concept itself.**
2. **The switch reads temperature, not the calendar.** The threshold is a surface
   temperature. A dark surface in February sun can cross it and turn bright, cancelling the
   gain we wanted — arguably self-limiting and acceptable, but it must be chosen
   deliberately. In the other direction, a summer morning starts dark and stays dark until
   the surface crosses the threshold, giving away part of the load window. **The switching
   temperature and the hysteresis width are design parameters, not product properties.**
3. **Is a step admissible for F9?** KPI 1 asks for a self-regulation gradient
   ∂P/∂T_s. A thermochromic surface has an almost infinite gradient *at* the switching
   point and roughly zero either side of it. That is a switch, not a proportional response.
   We have to decide whether F9 is satisfied by a threshold, or whether it requires a
   continuous response — and say why. This is a genuinely open question and answering it
   well is worth more than either answer.
4. **Where does the layer sit relative to the insulation?** If it sits outside a retrofit
   insulation layer, the winter heat it absorbs mostly returns to the outside air and never
   reaches the interior — the winter benefit largely evaporates. On an **uninsulated solid
   masonry wall**, which is what much of the protected Zurich stock actually is, the
   coupling is far better. **This determines which building stock the concept is for**, and
   it should be stated rather than assumed.
5. **Durability is the make-or-break.** Organic thermochromic dyes bleach under UV, and our
   KPI 7 demands ≥ 90 % of the function retained after three years, on a façade exposed to
   the full Swiss annual cycle. Ask for cycle life, not just switching range: how many
   switching cycles, and what does the switching range look like after n years of UV?
   Two routes out, both bio-inspired: a **UV filter over the switching layer** — the
   kynurenine mechanism from the eye lens dissipates UV energy through internal proton
   transfer instead of degrading, and is non-toxic and biodegradable, which our own
   run-off constraint requires — or **structural colour instead of a dye**, where the colour
   sits in the geometry and there is no molecule to bleach.

### How to make it testable

| Quantity | Formulation |
|---|---|
| Summer state | solar reflectance ≥ 0.80 for T<sub>s</sub> above the switching temperature (KPI 2) |
| Winter state | solar reflectance clearly below the 0.30 low-albedo class for T<sub>s</sub> below it |
| Switching temperature | T<sub>switch</sub> and hysteresis width, chosen and justified against the Zurich annual temperature distribution |
| Annual balance | summer benefit (PET reduction, KPI 4) against winter benefit (heating demand, kWh/m²a) — **one number, both seasons** |
| Durability | switching range retained after UV exposure / n cycles (KPI 7) |
| Glare | diffuse in the bright state; white is white, so the city's glare objection still applies |

### Why it is worth carrying anyway

It is the only option on the table that makes a **single passive surface do opposite things
in opposite seasons**, which is precisely what *adapt to changing conditions* means and what
a fixed high-albedo coating structurally cannot do. Even if the durability check kills the
dye route, the *principle* — seasonal switching — survives in routes 3 and 4, and the
question it forces ("must F9 be continuous, or is a threshold enough?") sharpens the whole
project.

## Concept option B — the self-deploying sun sail

**The idea.** A sail, awning or louvre field that **spreads itself when it gets hot and
retracts when it cools** — no motor, no sensor, no electricity. The heat that has to be kept
out is the same energy that does the work of keeping it out.

### Why this is the most literal answer to our design question

Our design question asks how the heat a building absorbs can **drive its own removal**. A
thermally actuated shade is powered by exactly the load it mitigates: the hotter it gets, the
more it deploys, the less arrives. That is F9 realised as **movement** rather than as a
material property — a third answer alongside the valve (Genius of Biome p. 63) and the
switchable coating (option A).

Three further advantages follow from the framing we adopted in Section 1.1:

1. **It acts in the street, not only at the wall.** A coating changes what the façade
   radiates; a sail removes direct sun from the person walking underneath. That addresses
   **KPI 4, PET at 2 m above ground** — the metric the city itself uses — directly rather
   than by inference.
2. **It solves the night conflict that defeats fixed shading.** Permanent shading lowers the
   sky view factor and therefore obstructs the long-wave emission we need after sunset: it
   buys a daytime benefit with a night-time penalty. A shade that **retracts as it cools**
   gets both — shade by day, open sky by night. This is precisely the gap we identified in
   HA 11.
3. **It resolves the summer/winter trade-off geometrically.** Retracted in winter means full
   passive solar gains — the same goal as option A, without a dye that bleaches under UV.

### Built precedents, both energy-free

| Precedent | Mechanism | Why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| **Thermobimetal** — Doris Sung, *InVert* shading system | two metals with different thermal expansion; the element curls in the sun and blocks light. Explicitly "no energy and no controls"; National Design Award 2021 (Climate Action), R&D Award 2020 | Proof that thermal self-actuation works on a building, at product level |
| **HygroSkin / HygroScope** — Menges, Krieg, Reichert | thin plywood apertures moved solely by the wood's moisture uptake, modelled on the **spruce cone**; closed at 30 % RH, fully open at 75 %, with no mechanics or electronics | Proof for the hygroscopic route, and the direct architectural use of the pine-cone strategy |
| AskNature: *Pine Cones Open and Close in Response to Weather* (strategy) and *Humidity-Sensitive Hydraulic Actuator Inspired by Pine Cones* (innovation) | bilayer of tissues with different swelling along the cellulose microfibril direction | The biological model and its abstracted actuator, both already documented |

### Technical routes (for the morphological matrix)

1. **Thermobimetal** — direct temperature actuation, robust, no chemistry, no consumables.
   Heaviest and most expensive per m².
2. **Shape-memory alloy (NiTi)** — actuates at a transition temperature with high force per
   mass; the heat is the input, so no auxiliary energy. Cost and fatigue are the questions.
3. **Hygroscopic bilayer** (wood, or 4D-printed biobased composite) — driven by humidity
   rather than temperature. Note the polarity is potentially favourable: it deploys when dry
   and furls when it rains, which also protects the element. But heat and humidity are only
   loosely correlated, so the coupling must be checked, not assumed.
4. **Wax or paraffin volume actuators** — a phase change drives a piston; cheap and
   high-force, but adds a sealed fluid element to a façade.

### The checks that decide it

1. **Wind — the make-or-break.** A sail across a street canyon is a structural and safety
   problem, and **a thermal actuator cannot sense wind**: a hot, stormy afternoon deploys it
   exactly when it must be furled. The concept therefore needs a **second, independent,
   equally passive release** — a mechanical fuse, a self-furling geometry, or perforation
   that lets gusts through. This is the critical engineering question for option B, and it is
   a good one.
2. **It has an incumbent.** Shading of the outdoor space near the building is the city's
   **HA 12**. Our novelty is not the shading; it is that the shading is **load-coupled and
   retracts at night**. The report must say this explicitly or the concept reads as a
   re-invention.
3. **Attachment to an existing, possibly listed façade.** Anchors are an intervention — but a
   textile or louvre field is **reversible**, which heritage authorities often prefer to a
   permanent coating. Both a constraint and an argument.
4. **Actuation temperature and the bee principle.** A single actuation point makes it
   all-or-nothing. Many elements with a **distribution** of actuation temperatures give a
   shaded fraction that grows continuously with the load — the same resolution the honeybee
   threshold model supplies for option A.
5. **What it does to the wall behind it.** Shading the façade reduces stored heat (helps F4
   and F5) but, while deployed, also reduces the wall's own sky view. Retraction resolves it;
   the size of both effects has to be estimated, not asserted.

### How to make it testable

| Quantity | Formulation |
|---|---|
| Deployment | shaded fraction of the target area as a function of surface or air temperature — the curve, not a single point |
| Response | actuation temperature range and hysteresis; time from onset of load to full deployment |
| Effect | PET reduction at 2 m under the deployed sail (KPI 4), against the HA 12 benchmark |
| Night | sky view factor at the façade, deployed vs retracted — the number that justifies the whole concept |
| Safety | wind speed at which the element furls, and by what independent mechanism |
| Energy | zero auxiliary energy — satisfied by construction for routes 1–4 |

### Why it is worth carrying

Of the four directions on the table it is the only one that is **passive, night-compatible,
seasonally correct and effective at pedestrian level at the same time**, and the only one
where the actuator is literally powered by the load. Its weakness is mechanical, not
physical — wind, attachment, fatigue — and mechanical weaknesses are the kind an engineering
check in Section 3.2 can actually address in a week.

## 4. Use life-friendly chemistry

Checklist items that apply:

- *uses a safe subset of the periodic table*
- *the breakdown of the design results only in by-products that cause no harm*
- *all chemistry is done using water as the solvent*
- *chemical reactions tap the power of self-assembly*

### Options

**4a — The biocide problem is a Life's-Principle problem.** Section 4 already lists
"biocides against algae" as an open material question. Under this principle it becomes
sharper: a surface that sheds biocide into the rainwater we then evaporate in the street
is releasing it at pedestrian level, in the exact place we are trying to improve. Whatever
keeps the surface clean (F10) must therefore be physical (structure, wetting behaviour)
rather than chemical, or it undermines the point of the project.
→ *Testable:* yes/no — does the build-up rely on a leachable biocide? A "no" is a
requirement, not a bonus.

**4b — Water as the solvent (slide 28).** The Bayer example — replacing petrochemical
solvents with water in polyurethane coatings — is the benchmark question to ask of any
coating we specify: is it water-borne?

**4c — Self-assembly (blue mussel, slide 27).** Relevant if a concept relies on a
structure that forms itself rather than being manufactured — see 5b.

## 5. Integrate development with growth — two usable readings, one trap

Checklist items: *components are modular and nested* · *built to shape (no cutting,
therefore no waste cuttings)* · *the components of the system are self-organizing* ·
*creates more opportunities (niches) for life*.

**5a — Built to shape.** A retrofit product cut on site to fit a façade produces offcuts.
A modular panel sized to a standard grid does not. This is a cheap, concrete,
checkable application.
→ *Testable:* offcut waste as a percentage of delivered area.

**5b — Self-organised structure.** Elephant skin — already in our model table — gets its
water-retaining micro-crack network by *drying*, not by machining. A layer whose
functional micro-structure forms itself during curing is self-organisation in the
checklist sense, and it is cheaper to make than an engraved one.

**5c — The trap: "creates more opportunities (niches) for life."** A permanently damp,
textured surface is an invitation to algae and moss — which is precisely the soiling that
KPI 7 penalises and F10 is meant to prevent. We should name this conflict rather than
claim the principle. Under a green-façade concept (HA 10) the niche is the point; under
ours it is a failure mode.

## 6. Evolve to survive — process, not product

Checklist items: *builds on what works* · *integrates the unexpected* · *cross-pollination
of information* · *mistakes are used to encourage continual idea generation*.

A façade layer does not evolve between generations. Claiming this principle for the
artefact would be decoration. It is honest, however, as a description of our **process**,
and Section 3.3 needs exactly that content anyway:

- *builds on what works* — we start from HA 11, the incumbent, rather than from zero;
- *cross-pollination* — the world café peer feedback and the instructor feedback;
- *integrates the unexpected* — the de-selection table, e.g. the termite-mound model
  rejected because the "chimney air-conditioning" account is contested;
- *mistakes encourage idea generation* — the V1 → engineering review → evidence → V2 chain.

## 7. Create conditions conducive to life (umbrella) — "optimised, not maximised"

The checklist's first line is *"all aspects and elements of the design are optimised rather
than maximised"*. For us that is not a platitude, it is a design decision we have already
half made: ultra-white paints reach a solar reflectance of ≈ 0.98, but our KPI 2 targets
≥ 0.80, the city's own threshold. Maximising reflectance would worsen glare — the objection
the city names first — and reduce winter solar gains and PV yield.

→ *Testable:* state the optimum, not the maximum, for albedo, and show what each further
0.05 of reflectance costs in glare, winter demand and PV yield.

## What this means for the report

1. **Section 2.7 currently mixes levels.** It lists "use low energy processes" and
   "maintain integrity through self-renewal" as if they were top-level principles; in the
   handbook they are sub-principles of *Be resource efficient* and *Adapt to changing
   conditions*. Restructure as: six named principles → the sub-principle we claim → the
   function it maps to → the KPI that measures it.
2. **Three principles are missing from our current list**: *Evolve to survive* (as process),
   *Integrate development with growth*, and the umbrella *Create conditions conducive to
   life*. Two of them we should explicitly decline, with the reason — the rubric rewards a
   justified de-selection and penalises a list.
3. **Each carried principle needs a KPI**, per slide 29. Five of the seven options above
   already map onto KPIs 1, 5, 6, 7 and 8. The two that do not — seasonal switching (3a)
   and the biocide question (4a) — are candidates for the Section 4 assessment table.
4. **Section 4 must assess, not list.** The rubric marks down "Life's Principles are listed
   rather than applied". The honest assessments are already visible: we apply *locally
   attuned* strongly, *resource efficient* partly, and we fail *creates niches for life*
   on purpose.

## Open decisions for the team

- **Concept option A, the colour-changing coating**: do we carry it as a second concept
  variant, or as a modifier on top of a removal concept? It cannot stand alone — it does
  nothing at night, which is the gap we identified. Deciding this also decides whether F9
  may be satisfied by a threshold rather than a continuous response.
- If we carry it: which building stock do we target? The winter half of the argument only
  works on an uninsulated wall.
- Do we require two independent removal paths (3b) as a design rule for the five-day case?
- Is the self-cleaning function allowed to be chemical at all (4a)?
