# AskNature *Innovations* — candidates for Challenge 1

Compiled 01.09.2026 from the AskNature Innovations catalogue.

## How to read this, and a warning about the sourcing

**AskNature blocks automated page access** (HTTP 403 on the Innovations listing and on
individual entries), so this list was assembled through search rather than by paging
through the catalogue. It is therefore **a good sample, not a complete sweep** — and the
descriptions below come from search summaries plus the primary sources behind them
(PNAS, Nature Communications, university press releases, manufacturer data), not from
reading each AskNature page. **Open every entry you intend to use and verify it**, which
is also what the course rule demands: AskNature is a discovery tool, the primary paper is
the evidence.

**The methodological point that matters more than any single entry:** AskNature separates
*Biological Strategies* from *Innovations*. Our Section 3.1 model table needs **strategies**
— organism, mechanism, evidence. The **innovations** below are something else, and they are
useful in two other places:

1. **The third column of the abstraction table** (`03_bioinspired_design.tex:82`,
   "possible technical interpretation"), which is currently empty. An innovation is proof
   that a given abstraction *can* be built.
2. **The state of the art** (Section 2.3). Several entries below are commercial products or
   funded prototypes that do part of what we propose. Not citing them would be a gap a
   grader can find in one search.

## 1 · Camel-fur bilayer passive cooler — the best find

MIT researchers built a **two-layer passive cooler**: an inner **hydrogel** from which water
evaporates (the sweat gland) under an outer **aerogel** that blocks incoming heat while
letting water vapour pass (the camel's fur). Total thickness under half an inch.

**Why this matters more than anything else on the list.** The counter-intuitive biology is
that a camel keeps *more* insulation on in the heat, and the measured consequence is
*metering*: the bilayer held its sample **7 °C below ambient for 200 hours** before needing
recharging — **five times longer than the hydrogel alone**.

That is our F8 — "meter evaporation so the reserve lasts the whole heat wave" — with a
number attached. Our KPI 6 asks for **≥ 5 days of autonomy**; 200 hours is 8.3 days. The
mechanism that buys the factor of five is *an insulating layer over the wet layer*, which is
the opposite of what one would design intuitively (expose the wet surface to the air).

**What to check before using it:** the 200 h figure is for a small cooled sample in a
laboratory, not a façade in a street canyon, and it measures *how long a reserve lasts*, not
*how much heat leaves per m²*. Our F8 has to do both. Also: what is the aerogel's cost and
its resistance to frost and driving rain?

**Where it goes:** the morphological matrix, as an option under "retain and meter water",
and the abstraction table as the technical interpretation of the camel strategy.

## 2 · Lotus-Effect façade paint — the state of the art we are missing

**StoColor Lotusan**, on the market since **1999**. A micro-structured superhydrophobic
façade coating: water beads and rolls off, carrying dirt with it. The manufacturer states it
resists algae and fungal growth **without biocide film protection**, is UV-stable with good
colour retention, has high water-vapour permeability, and extends cleaning and re-coating
cycles.

**Why this is important for us in three separate ways:**

1. It is **prior art on our own client's problem**. The City of Zurich names soiling as the
   specific weakness of high-albedo envelopes; a commercial biomimetic façade paint has
   addressed exactly that for twenty-five years. Our Section 2.3 does not mention it.
2. It **proves the physical route works commercially**. Our life-friendly-chemistry analysis
   argues that F10 must be structural rather than chemical. Lotusan is the existence proof —
   which converts our argument from a preference into a benchmark.
3. It gives **KPI 7 a real reference**: instead of inventing "≥ 90 % after three years", we
   can ask what Lotusan actually achieves and set our target against it.

**What to check:** the manufacturer's own durability figures, and the independent literature
on how lotus-effect façade coatings age — self-cleaning coatings are known to lose
performance as the microstructure wears or fills. That literature is also the honest
counterweight to point 3.

## 3 · Longhorn-beetle radiative cooling film

*Neocerambyx gigas* survives near active volcanoes at up to **70 °C**; triangular structures
on its wings simultaneously scatter sunlight and emit thermal radiation. UT Austin with
Shanghai Jiao Tong and KTH reproduced this in **PDMS with embedded ceramic particles,
micro-stamped with the triangular pattern**, and measured items under the film at up to
**5.1 °C cooler** than without. Published in PNAS (2020); reported to reflect ~95 % of solar
radiation.

**Why it fits us:** it does F1 and F2 in one layer — prevention and removal, which is exactly
the combination our design question demands and which a bright paint cannot deliver. And it
is explicitly the *scalable* answer to the standard objection that photonic radiative coolers
need high-precision, low-yield manufacturing.

**The caution:** 5.1 °C is far below our KPI 3 target of ≥ 15 K peak surface temperature
reduction — but the two numbers are not comparable (different reference, different
measurement). Do not put them in the same sentence without checking what each measured.

## 4 · HydroCanopy — the closest prior art to our own concept

A façade system inspired by **the mucous glands in the epidermis of frogs and toads** and by
**the density-and-size gradient of leaves in a tree canopy**. It regulates solar radiation,
ventilation and thermal inertia and adds evaporative cooling — a multi-layer, co-dependent
system, using local and abundant materials, aimed at regions without access to
energy-intensive cooling. A 2021 Biomimicry Global Design Challenge finalist.

**Read this one carefully before we develop our concepts.** It combines the same four
functions we combine, from the same two directions (an evaporating skin and a graded canopy).
Two consequences: we must cite it, and we must be able to say what our concept does that it
does not. Our honest answer is **F9** — the self-regulating coupling — and the **night
window**, neither of which its description claims.

## 5 · Squid-skin adaptive infrared blanket — the night-time version of our colour idea

UC Irvine (Gorodetsky, Leung), *Nature Communications* 2019. A material with tiny **metal
islands**: relaxed, they sit together and the sheet reflects and traps infrared like a normal
space blanket; **stretched, the islands separate and infrared passes through**. Reported
performance: transmittance on/off ratio ≈ 25, regulates a heat flux of **≈ 36 W/m²** with
**≈ 3 W/m² of mechanical power input**, and a dynamic setpoint window of ≈ 8 °C.

**Why this belongs next to Concept option A.** Our thermochromic coating switches *solar
absorptance* and therefore does nothing at night. This switches **infrared emissivity**,
which is the night-time path — the exact gap we identified in HA 11. As a pair, the two
options are the same idea applied to the two halves of the spectrum.

**The constraint it violates, and the question that follows.** It needs ~3 W/m² of mechanical
input, and our energy constraint is hard: no auxiliary energy for the cooling function. So
the design question becomes: **can the same geometric switch be actuated passively** — by
thermal expansion, by a hygroscopic layer that swells when wet, by the drying of a gel? That
is a genuinely good critical engineering question, and the pine cone and the *Selaginella*
resurrection plant are the obvious places to look for a passive actuator.

## 6 · Super-white material from *Cyphochilus* — a model we already have

*Cyphochilus* scales are only **7 ± 1.5 µm thick** yet reflect **65–70 %**, with no pigment —
brightness from the geometry of a dense chitin network. **Seprify** commercialises this in
**cellulose**, reported as 20× whiter than paper, as a non-toxic replacement for **titanium
dioxide** (TiO₂, whose nanoparticle form the EU has banned as a food additive).

**Why it is useful:** *Cyphochilus* is **already in our model table** via Vukusic 2007. This
innovation is its missing third column — the technical interpretation — and it hands us a
life-friendly-chemistry argument for free: structural white avoids the TiO₂ question
entirely, and our KPI 2 (≥ 0.80 solar reflectance) then has a route that is not a pigment.

## 7 · Cypris Materials — structural colour, tunable into the near infrared

Self-assembling **brush block copolymers** that form layers reflecting light; dissolved in a
carrier such as a UV-curable monomer, so it is **paintable**. By tuning, UV or **near-infrared**
can be reflected. About 50 % plant-based raw materials. The initial development was seeded by
a **$5M ARPA-E grant with the explicit goal of paintable reflective coatings that reduce heat
intake in buildings** — i.e. our application, funded as such.

Relevant to Concept option A in a way we had not considered: structural colour is set by a
*spacing*, and a spacing can in principle be changed by swelling, stretching or drying. A
structural-colour coating whose spacing shifts with temperature or humidity would be a
passive switch that avoids the UV-bleaching problem of leuco dyes — which was the
make-or-break check on that option.

## 8 · Chameleon-inspired colour-changing material

Core–shell structured layers giving a continuous range of vivid colours; the material also
acts as a chemosensor, **changing colour in response to temperature or pH**. This is the
nearest AskNature entry to Concept option A's core claim, and worth reading for how it
handles reversibility and cycle life.

## 12 · Eastgate Centre — cite it, then de-select it

The Harare building is the textbook termite-mound case and it will come up in the world café.
Our Section 2 already de-selects the termite model on evidence (Turner 2008), and the
building itself supports that: its description involves **fans on a cycle** to enhance heat
storage by day and release at night. It is therefore not a passive system, and under our
hard energy constraint it is out. Saying this in one sentence, with both citations, is worth
more than ignoring it.

## 13 · Pitcher-plant coating — the counter-example

**spotLESS** is a sprayable coating with a **liquid-infused nanosponge** based on the pitcher
plant's slippery surface. It is the technically elegant option for F10 — and it is the one
our life-friendly-chemistry analysis should question, because a liquid-infused surface works
by holding a lubricant that can be depleted or washed into the run-off we intend to harvest
and evaporate at street level. Worth one line as a rejected alternative with a stated reason.

## What to do with this

1. **Section 2.3 (state of the art)** gains two entries it currently lacks: **Lotusan**
   (F10 solved commercially, without biocide) and **HydroCanopy** (an evaporative bio-inspired
   façade already built as a prototype). Both are cases where an examiner could otherwise ask
   "did you know this exists?".
2. **The abstraction table** (`03_bioinspired_design.tex:82`) can be filled: for each
   biological model we keep, the innovations above supply the third column, technical
   interpretation — *Cyphochilus* → Seprify, silver ant → the longhorn-beetle film, elephant
   skin → the camel bilayer's metering logic.
3. **The morphological matrix** gains real options per function rather than invented ones:
   F1 (structural white · NIR-tuned structural colour · micro-stamped photonic film),
   F2 (broadband emitter · switchable IR window), F8 (hydrogel under an aerogel · fog-capture
   textile · moss-like double layer).
4. **Concept option A gets a sibling and a better route.** The sibling is the squid-skin
   switch on the emission side; the better route is structural colour tuned by swelling
   rather than a bleaching dye.
5. **Two new critical engineering questions**, both good enough for Section 3.2:
   - Can a geometric IR switch be actuated **passively**, without the ~3 W/m² the squid-skin
     material needs?
   - Does an insulating layer **over** the evaporating layer extend our reserve the way it
     extends the camel bilayer's — and by how much, in our climate?
