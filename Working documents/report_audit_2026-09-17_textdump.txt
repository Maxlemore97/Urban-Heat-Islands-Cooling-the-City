     5	# Introduction
     6	
     7	## Background & Relevance
     8	
     9	The Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect represents a growing environmental and public health challenge, characterized by significantly elevated temperatures in urban environments relative to surrounding rural areas \[1\]. This effect is worsened by climate change and a high concentration of heat absorbing infrastructure, like cement and asphalt. Conventional mitigation strategies rely heavily on air conditioning, which creates a counterproductive cycle. It increases external temperatures through heat rejection and increases energy consumption, often causing higher greenhouse gas emissions.
    10	
    11	To address this problem, the report investigates nature inspired passive cooling mechanisms, also known as biomimicry.
    12	
    13	## Design Challenge & Project Contribution
    14	
    15	The design challenge lies in developing an efficient, scalable cooling system capable of mitigating the urban heat island effect without relying on high energy consumption.
    16	
    17	This project aims to find a solution which bridges the gap between thermal regulation and sustainable urban infrastructure. The goal is to analyze the different heat dissipation methods found in nature, specifically in animals, to find a solution which conforms with the given challenges.
    18	
    19	# Challenge & Scoping
    20	
    21	## Problem Definition
    22	
    23	Conventional cooling methods such as air condition worsen the Urban Heat Island effect by displacing the warm air from inside the building to the surrounding area. This local heat exchange causes the city to warm up more, increasing the need for air condition which ultimately results in a negative feedback loop.
    24	
    25	To break this cycle a new alternative and sustainable solution needs to be found.
    26	
    27	## Context and Constraints
    28	
    29	All context values, benchmarks and engineering checks in this report refer to the City of Zurich. Fixing one reference city is what makes the constraints and the KPI targets checkable. Zürich was chosen for four reasons:
    30	
    31	1.  The city has an adopted a heat-mitigation strategy, the Fachplanung Hitzeminderung [(Stadt Zürich, 2020)]{.mark}, with an implementation agenda, a guidance leaflet for building owners and thematics maps. The project therefore has a documented baseline and published target values instead of invented ones.
    32	
    33	2.  The city publishes an open climate analysis and including geodata, so the reference site can be selected from measured data instead of intuition.
    34	
    35	3.  MeteoSwiss operates long term measurement stations in and around the city.
    36	
    37	4.  Its building style and infrastructure are representative of the Swiss Mittelland, so results transfer to comparable Swiss cities.
    38	
    39	### Reference Building
    40	
    41	For comparison and reference purposes a building inside of Zurich was chosen. The chosen building is the Modellierungsgebiet 03 - Geschlossene Randbebauung (closed perimeter block). It consists of two outward-closed blocks of five stories including a roof structure and an enclosed private courtyard. It is located in a dense district close to the city center. The city's assessment is that the reduced air exchange and lack of cold air supply makes this building overheat comparatively quickly. Using this building as context for the report allows for a more accurate and realistic project grounded in a realistic setting.

   202	## Performance Criteria and KPIs
   203	
   204	### KPIs
   205	
   206	**#1 Albedo Index**
   207	
   208	The Albedo index defines a value between 0 -- 1 where 0 is total absorption of all incoming radiation and 1 is the total reflection of all incoming radiation. \[2\]
   209	
   210	Therefore we can use the Albedo index as measurement unit to compare the reflective properties of our materials.
   211	
   212	**#2 Time spent in the shade**
   213	
   214	Shading provides an alternative way to keep a surface cool. \[3\] Therefore the aim of this KPI is to keep a surface under shade for as long as possible. The units of measurement for this KPI will therefore be minutes spent in the shade.
   215	
   216	**#3 Heat dissipated**
   217	
   218	This KPI aims at measuring how well heat is dissipated from a surface. This can be measured by heat dissipated (°C) in a set amount of time.
   219	
   220	**#4 Heat transferred**
   221	
   222	This KPI aims at measuring how well heat is transferred from one surface to another using any medium as transfer. This can be measured by heat transferred (°C) in a set amount of time.
   223	
   224	**#5 Water evaporated**
   225	
   226	Water evaporation is an effective solution to remove heat using moisture as means of heat transfer. This KPI uses the amount of water (liter) necessary to remove a set amount of temperature. The aim is to reduce the needed amount of water evaporation to conserve water.
   227	
   228	**#6 Efficiency factor**
   229	
   230	This KPI measures the longevity of the solution by using a efficiency factor in percentage. The goal is to keep the factor as high as possible for as long as possible.
   231

   276	### Natural Models
   277	
   278	#### African Elephant: Ears
   279	
   280	**Biological mechanism**
   281	
   282	As seen in Figure 1 African elephants have large and highly vascularized ears which they use as heat exchange surfaces. Warm blood is transported from the body core into the ear, where heat is transferred to the surrounding air. By flapping their ears additional airflow is created which further increases the heat transfer. \[4\] This same approach is also used by the jackrabbit. \[5\]
   283	
   284	![Figure 3.1 African Elephant ears](media/image1.png){width="4.169811898512686in" height="2.3456714785651793in"}
   285	
   286	**Functional abstraction.** Transfer thermal energy from a heat generating region to a large, exchange surface and increase airflow across this surface.
   287	
   288	**Design principle for urban cooling.** Use dedicated heat rejection surfaces in locations with favorable airflow to transfer and dissipate heat from urban structures.
   289	
   290	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions
   291	
   292	#### Elephant Skin: Sparse Hairs
   293	
   294	**Biological mechanism**
   295	
   296	African elephants have additionally to their ears a second cooling mechanism. They have very sparsely spread hair all over their body. Due to the low density of the body hair it does not act as an insulation layer but rather as additional surface are to transfer heat. As seen in Figure 3.2 it also serves to break the stationary air surrounding the skin. This allows for a better heat transfer. \[6\]
   297	
   298	![Figure 3.2 African elephant hair](media/image2.png){width="4.834332895888014in" height="2.782609361329834in"}
   299	
   300	**Functional abstraction.** Increase heat-transfer area by distributing sparse bulges across a warm surface.
   301	
   302	**Design principle for urban cooling.** Use small surface structures on façades or other heated urban surfaces to enhance passive heat transfer without substantially increasing material use.
   303	
   304	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient
   305	
   306	#### Cactus Ribs
   307	
   308	**Biological mechanism**
   309	
   310	Many cacti have ribbed surfaces consisting of repeated peaks and troughs. The peaks shade parts of the troughs and therefore reduce direct solar exposure. Air within the shaded troughs remains cooler and absorbs heat from the cactus which rises as it warms. The ribbed geometry also increases surface area and disrupts airflow around the plant, similar to the elephant hair. \[7\]
   311	
   312	**Functional abstraction**
   313	
   314	Use folded surface geometry to combine self-shading, increased heat-transfer area and controlled interaction with airflow.
   315	
   316	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   317	
   318	Integrate ribbed or folded geometries into urban surfaces to reduce direct solar exposure while simultaneously promoting passive heat dissipation.
   319	
   320	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient
   321	
   322	#### Saharan Silver Ant: Reflective Hairs
   323	
   324	**Biological Mechanism**
   325	
   326	The Saharan silver ant is covered with densely packed hairs that have a triangular cross-section and grooved surfaces. These hairs reduce heat gain in two ways: they strongly reflect visible and near-infrared solar radiation through total internal reflection and they increase emissivity in the mid-infrared range, allowing the ant to radiate excess body heat more efficiently. AskNature reports that the hairs alone keep the ant about 2°C cooler, while the combined mechanisms provide an additional cooling effect of approximately 5--10°C. \[8\]
   327	
   328	**Functional abstraction**
   329	
   330	Use micro structured surfaces to reflect incoming solar radiation while simultaneously enhancing thermal radiation away from the surface.
   331	
   332	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   333	
   334	Develop façade or roof surfaces with micro structured geometries that minimize absorption in the visible and near-infrared solar spectrum while maximizing heat emission in the mid-infrared. This could reduce solar heat gain and improve passive radiative cooling without requiring active energy input.
   335	
   336	**Life's Principle:** Adapt to changing conditions / Use life-friendly chemistry
   337	
   338	#### Desert Snail Shell
   339	
   340	**Biological mechanism**
   341	
   342	The desert snail Sphincterochila boissieri limits heat gain through a highly reflective shell and internal insulating air spaces as seen in Figure 3.3. AskNature reports approximately 90 % reflectance in the visible spectrum and about 95 % in the near-infrared range. The snail also withdraws into the upper parts of its shell, while air-filled spaces and limited contact with the hot substrate reduce conductive heat transfer. \[9\]
   343	
   344	![Figure 3.3 Desert Snail \[9\]](media/image3.jpeg){width="3.993740157480315in" height="3.4469695975503063in"}
   345	
   346	**Functional abstraction**
   347	
   348	Reduce incoming radiant energy and interrupt conductive heat transfer using reflective surfaces and stationary air layers.
   349	
   350	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   351	
   352	Combine highly reflective external surfaces with insulating or ventilated air cavities to limit solar heat storage in roofs and façades.
   353	
   354	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions
   355	
   356	#### Morpho Butterfly Wing
   357	
   358	**Biological mechanism**
   359	
   360	Morpho butterflies generate their characteristic color using transparent chitin-and-air structures at micro- and nanoscale. As seen in Figure 3.4, ridges and cross-ribs interact with incoming light through diffraction and interference, causing selected wavelengths to be strongly reflected. The color therefore results primarily from physical structure rather than pigmentation. \[10\]
   361	
   362	![Figure 3.4 Reflection with nano structures \[11\]](media/image4.png){width="4.9810203412073495in" height="2.8030293088363956in"}
   363	
   364	**Functional abstraction**
   365	
   366	Control the spectral interaction between a surface and incoming radiation through micro- and nanoscale geometry.
   367	
   368	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   369	
   370	Engineer structured surfaces that selectively manipulate incident solar radiation while maintaining desired optical properties. I needs to be mentioned that the Morpho demonstrates structural color rather than near-infrared heat rejection and that cooling application requires separate verification.
   371	
   372	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient
   373	
   374	#### Camel Fur and Sweat
   375	
   376	**Biological mechanism**
   377	
   378	Dromedary camels combine insulation with evaporative cooling. Figure 3.4 displays how exactly this mechanism works. The sweat glands release water which removes the body heat while evaporation and the thick fur blocks the hot air from reaching the skin. Furthermore the camel reflects light with their pale color. \[12\]
   379	
   380	![Figure 3.4 Insulation with evaporative cooling \[12\]](media/image5.jpeg){alt="A diagram showing a section of camel fur and how it slows heat gain from light, air, and the environment." width="5.160377296587926in" height="3.372127077865267in"}
   381	
   382	**Functional abstraction**
   383	
   384	Block heat using insolation in combination with evaporation as a means of heat dissipation.
   385	
   386	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   387	
   388	Combine shading or thermal insulation with controlled evaporative cooling so that less water is required to achieve the desired cooling effect.
   389	
   390	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions / Use life-friendly chemistry
   391	
   392	#### Pine Cone Scales
   393	
   394	**Biological mechanism**
   395	
   396	Pine cone scales respond to humidity despite containing no muscles or active control system. Their layered structure contains materials with different swelling behavior. When moisture is absorbed, one layer expands more than the other, causing the scale to bend and close. As the scale dries, it returns towards its open state. The movement is reversible. \[13\]
   397	
   398	**Functional abstraction**
   399	
   400	Convert an environmental change directly into mechanical movement through differential material expansion.
   401	
   402	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   403	
   404	Use environmentally responsive material layers to create self-actuating shading or ventilation elements that operate without motors or electronic control.
   405	
   406	**Life's Principle:** Adapt to changing conditions / Use life-friendly chemistry
   407	
   408	#### Honeybee Fanning Thresholds
   409	
   410	**Biological mechanism**
   411	
   412	Western honeybee colonies regulate nest temperature through the combined behavior of many workers. Individual bees have different temperature-response thresholds. As nest temperature increases, progressively more bees begin ventilating the hive. This distributed response prevents excessive system-wide reactions to small temperature fluctuations and stabilizes the nest with no central control. \[14\]
   413	
   414	**Functional abstraction**
   415	
   416	Distribute different activation thresholds across multiple independent elements to create a gradual system-level response.
   417	
   418	**Design principle for urban cooling**
   419	
   420	Use multiple passive cooling elements with different trigger conditions so that cooling capacity increases progressively with thermal load instead of operating through a single on/off threshold.
   421	
   422	**Life's Principle:** Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions

   542	The chosen water-temperature differences, 10 °C and 3 °C, test stronger and weaker driving conditions; they are not measured site values or comfort targets. A smaller difference weakens buoyancy while requiring more flow to carry the same heat load.

   544	Heat rate Q̇ is energy carried per second; ṁ is water mass flow. Assume steady liquid flow and approximate the hot/cold leg difference by the water temperature change ΔT. Equations use kelvin (K): a difference of 10 K equals a change of 10 °C.

   546	**Q̇ = A~f~ qʺ ≈ 1,220 m² × 100 W/m² = 122 kW**

   549	
   550	**Δp~loss~ = (f L/D + ΣK) ρv²/2 ; v = 4ṁ/(ρπD²)**

   552	Here D is internal diameter, v water speed and g = 9.81 m/s². Use Darcy friction factor f = 0.3164/Re^0.25^, with Re = ρvD/μ, and solve where friction equals buoyancy \[17\]. Re ≈ 26,000--39,000 supports the smooth-pipe turbulent-flow approximation.

   566	**3.2.8 Engineering Check 2 --- Roof Heat Release**
   567	
   568	In steady operation, the roof must release the heat collected by the façade. Raman et al. (2014) measured 40.1 W/m² net cooling at air temperature under sunlight above 850 W/m² \[18\]. This specialised emitter provides a benchmark, not an assigned property of our coating.
   569	
   570	**A~release~ = Q̇ / qʺ~net~**
   571	
   572	For the engineering estimate, assume a 35 °C roof, 30 °C air, effective sky temperature 20 °C, solar reflectance r = 0.95, thermal emittance ε = 0.90, sunlight G = 800 W/m² and convection coefficient h = 3--8 W/(m²·K). Effective sky temperature represents incoming infrared radiation, not air temperature. Neglect back-surface exchange and assume an unobstructed sky:
   573	
   574	**qʺ~net~ = h(T~roof~ − T~air~) + εσ(T~roof~⁴ − T~sky~⁴) − (1 − r)G**
   575	
   576	The terms represent heat to air, infrared heat exchange and absorbed sunlight. Fourth powers require absolute temperature: T \[K\] = T \[°C\] + 273.15; thus 35 °C = 308.15 K. With σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴), net release is 15--40 + 83 − 40 ≈ 58--83 W/m². Roof tilt and nearby buildings would change this estimate.

   728	# Sustainability & Life's Principles
   729	
   730	## Life's Principles applied to Concept V2
   731	
   732	### Be resource efficient --- minimize the use of external resources
   733	
   734	**How Concept V2 applies it.** Cooling needs no auxiliary electricity: circulation is driven by buoyancy alone (Section 3.2.7), and the loop is closed, so no water is consumed in operation (Table 3.10).
   735	
   736	**Where it falls short.** Pumpless operation is not demonstrated. The mains alone use most of the ≈300 Pa buoyancy pressure, the drawn roof coils exceed it by a factor of about 67, and refilling after drainback may need a pump (Section 3.3.5).
   737	
   738	### Adapt to changing conditions --- day/night and seasonal cycles
   739	
   740	**How Concept V2 applies it.** The daily cycle is used rather than fought: a hotter façade produces a larger density difference and more flow, so cooling rises and falls with the load without a controller (Section 3.2.7), and the roof releases most of its heat to the night sky (Section 3.2.8). The seasonal cycle is met by draining the loop before frost and refilling it in spring (Section 3.3.5).
   741	
   742	**Where it falls short.** The seasonal adaptions rather functions as a withdrawal and not adaptation. Even after draining the pipes there is a remain risk of freezing leftover water. Furthermore the Ribs and reflective roof act the same even in the winter.
   743	
   744	### Use life-friendly chemistry --- no toxic chemicals or materials
   745	
   746	**How Concept V2 applies it.** The heat-transfer fluid is plain water. Frost protection is solved by draining the pipes instead of anti freeze such as glycol. Reflection and emission are sought through the surface optics of the silver ant \[22\], not through a functional additive.
   747	
   748	**Where it falls short.** The principle is met by exclusion, not by verification. Two points remain open and unchecked: the timber treatment of the ribs and the binder and preservatives of the roof coating.
   749	
   750	## Environmental benefits, limitations and trade-offs
   751	
   752	### Environmental benefits
   753	
   754	**Displaced cooling load.** The climate benefit is not what V2 uses but what it replaces: every hour of air conditioning it makes unnecessary is avoided emissions and avoided waste heat in the street (Section 2.1).
   755	
   756	**Heat exported, not moved.** Radiation to the night sky carries the collected heat out of the urban air; the other release options of Table 3.5, exchange with the air beside the wall or evaporation, leave it in the street (Section 3.2.8).
   757	
   758	**A cooler street.** The shaded façade radiates less onto the pavement, there is no condenser at pedestrian height, and reflection happens on the roof, where it cannot raise heat stress in the canyon \[15\].
   759	
   760	**A reversible intervention.** Nothing is demolished; the panels can be removed and the building returned to its previous state, so the retrofit creates no demolition waste and consumes no land.
   761	
   762	**Nothing emitted in operation.** No water, no refrigerant, no additive and no noise leave the system while it runs.
   763	
   764	### Limitations
   765	
   766	**A fair-weather benefit.** The export of heat depends on a clear, dry night sky. In the humid, hazy nights of a heat wave most of the heat only warms the roof air, so the concept helps least in the conditions it is meant for (Section 3.2.8).
   767	
   768	**Idle for most of the year.** Outside the summer the drained loop does nothing, and no other environmental function fills the gap.
   769	
   770	**A benefit without a number.** No cooling figure exists, so the avoided emissions cannot be counted, and the whole benefit rests on circulation without a pump, the first open check of Table 3.13.
   771	
   772	**A balance with one side.** The added materials carry embodied emissions that no life cycle assessment has set against the avoided cooling load (Table 2.1).
   773	
   774	### Trade-offs
   775	
   776	**Townscape.** Ribs and panels change how the building looks; on a listed façade this collides with heritage protection \[19, S. 148--151\].
   777	
   778	**Winter heating.** What shades and reflects in summer also rejects winter sun and can raise heating demand and its emissions \[23\]; the net annual effect is unknown.
   779	
   780	**Roof area.** Every square metre that releases heat is a square metre without photovoltaic yield (Figure 3.4).
   781	
   782	**Additive or downtime.** Avoiding antifreeze costs the shoulder seasons and admits air that may bring back a corrosion inhibitor (Section 3.3.5).
   783	
   784	**Access or invisibility.** Repairable external pipes are the same visible layer that alters the façade.
   785	
   786	Under a clear summer sky V2 exports heat from the city at no running cost; the rest of the year it is a visible layer on a listed façade that yields nothing.
   787	
   788	## Materials, durability, repair and circularity
   789	
   790	**Timber ribs.** Worn by UV, driving rain and freeze--thaw, so they belong to the biological cycle. What weathers off lands on the pavement, and the timber must be untreated, naturally durable, or protected by detailing rather than by chemistry.
   791	
   792	**Collector panels and finish.** Slow surface wear, otherwise used as structure: technical cycle, if the panel is one recoverable material with a benign finish. The panels are designed to be removable (Figure 3.8).
   793	
   794	**Roof coating.** Worn by UV, rain, soiling and cleaning, and abraded into run-off that reaches the stormwater network of Section 2.3, so it must break down harmlessly.
   795	
   796	**Pipework, carrier plate, supports and fixings.** Used, not consumed: technical cycle, recoverable if specified as separable single materials.
   797	
   798	**Water in the loop.** Used, but mobile, and in neither cycle cleanly: about 5.8 m³ is drained to storage each winter and eventually disposed of, and with an inhibitor it is the one component that can leave the system by accident.
   799	
   800	Measured against the eight cycle design principles, V2 answers three in principle. Modular construction (3) and reparability (5) follow from moving the pipes out of the wall: a panel can be removed and the façade pipes inspected without opening the wall (Sections 3.3.2 to 3.3.4). Ecotoxic chemicals (1) and recyclable materials (2) are answered above.
   801	
   802	**Material checked so far.** White cool-roof coatings owe their reflectance to titanium dioxide, so it was checked in the REACH database of the European Chemicals Agency \[27\]. The powder form was classified in 2020 as a suspected carcinogen by inhalation (Regulation (EU) 2020/217, H351). The General Court annulled the classification in 2022 and the Court of Justice confirmed the annulment on 1 August 2025 \[28\], so the pigment currently carries no harmonized hazard classification. The result is not clear. The concern was dust inhalation, not the applied film, and the ruling turned on the reliability of one study, not on a finding of safety. The pigment is also not the used coating. The binder and the film preservatives are what reach the run-off instead. One substance of at least four has been checked. The other three belong to item 5 of Table 3.13.
   803	
   804	## Did sustainability considerations change the design?
   805	
   806	The design was adjusted in four instances which were documented in sections 3.2 and 3.3.
   807	
   808	**1. The reflective surface moved from the façade to the roof.** Concept C reflected on the façade and was set aside because reflective walls raised pedestrian heat stress in simulations of a Stuttgart street canyon \[15\]; Concept A reflects on the roof instead (Section 3.2.2). The change was made for the people at street level, not for cooling performance.
   809	
   810	**2. Water and power demand removed two options before any concept was drawn.** The evaporating wet layer and the fan-driven cavity of Table 3.5 were not developed further because of their water and power demand (Section 3.2.2). The Energy and Water constraints of Table 2.1, which are be resource efficient written as constraints, decided this.
   811	
   812	**3. The pipes moved out of the wall.** V1 embedded them in the façade. The engineering review found that in case of damage the lack of access was unacceptable. (Table 3.11), and Therefore V2 placed them in a separable external panels with a drainage gap behind them (Section 3.3.3).
   813	
   814	**4. Water instead of glycol.** Frost protection was decided by drainback rather than by an additive (Section 3.3.5).
   815	
   816	## Limits of this assessment
   817	
   818	No life cycle assessment was performed, so nothing in this section is a quantified environmental balance. The assessment is made against Concept V2 as described in Section 3.3, whose central claim of buoyancy-only circulation is the first-ranked open check of Table 3.13; if a pump is needed, the Energy constraint and the first row of Table 4.1 fall with it. Durability is defined by KPI 6 but has no data, cascade use and return logistics lie outside the project boundary, and of the substances the concept would put into the street one has been checked and three have not.

   820	# Discussion & Conclusion
   821	
   822	## Contribution of the biomimicry process
   823	
   824	Biomimicry helped us explore cooling functions before committing to familiar building solutions. Studying how organisms manage heat encouraged us to abstract and combine strategies, leading to the proposed link between façade shading, fluid transport and a roof emitter (Sections 3.1--3.2). Without this process, we would probably have focused on improving existing solutions individually. Conventional engineering could produce a similar concept, but the biological models broadened our search and helped us connect these functions.
   825	
   826	## Strengths and weaknesses of the final concept
   827	
   828	V2 offers a unconventional approach for new buildings and especially retrofits: shading is intended to limit façade heating, while the water loop transfers collected heat to the roof. This aims to reduce heat release at street level without relying on reflective façades that could increase exposure for pedestrians and neighboring buildings. Modular panels could be installed where cooling is most needed. Heat-driven circulation and a closed loop offer the prospect of operation without continuous pumping or intentional evaporative water use. However, circulation and the available roof area are the main limitations. The calculated pipe resistance exceeds the available driving pressure, requiring a revised network with shorter parallel circuits (Section 3.3.5). Pump-free circulation remains unverified, and refilling may require a pump. Testing must establish whether the revised system delivers the intended cooling.
   829	
   830	## Assessment of the challenge and the KPIs
   831	
   832	The concept addresses the façade's contribution to urban heat; it does not address all causes of the wider challenge. The KPIs guided design decisions: heat-flow estimates limited the connected façade area, and the water constraint favored a loop without intentional evaporation (Section 3.3). They have therefore supported concept development, but target achievement has not been demonstrated. Reflectance, shading duration, durability and actual cooling performance still need verification. Winter operation, glare and heritage compatibility also remain open. These gaps define the next evaluation steps.
   833	
   834	## Innovative character and limits of the evidence
   835	
   836	The innovative aspect is the combination of biological principles in a maintainable façade system intended for new buildings and retrofits. We do not claim that its components are new. Our main lesson from the course is that biological ideas become more useful when calculations and practical constraints shape their technical transfer. The estimates support further development, but do not establish measured cooling performance. No life-cycle assessment was performed. Circulation and roof-output tests are the priorities for assessing the concept's potential.
