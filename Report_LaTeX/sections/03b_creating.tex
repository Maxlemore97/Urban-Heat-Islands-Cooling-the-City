\textbf{A = S1--O1--T1--E1} combines fixed fins, a reflective roof and a water loop.
Variable connected area (Crazy 8s, Idea~7) remains an unselected regulation option for
\textbf{A}.

\textbf{B = T2--E2} uses a textured coating for local heat exchange.

\textbf{C = S2--O2--E2} combines responsive louvers with a reflective façade.

The strategies listed under Option~3 in Table~\ref{tab:morph} were not selected because
of water or power demand, uncertain optical performance, or insulation restricting
outward heat transfer. Fans and cladding are conventional technical implementations.

\textbf{C} was set aside because humidity does not directly indicate cooling demand and
reflective façades also increased pedestrian heat stress in simulations of a Stuttgart
street canyon \cite{lee2018}. \textbf{A} therefore places its reflective surface on the
roof.

\textbf{A} and \textbf{B} were retained for the engineering comparison below.

\subsubsection{Concept A V1 --- Water Façade}
\label{sec:conceptA}

\textbf{A}~V1 was conceived for a new building, with water pipes embedded in the façade
and timber fins providing shade (Figure~\ref{fig:conceptA}). The water loop carries
residual heat to a separate roof surface for release.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/fig_conceptA_v1.jpg}
\caption{Concept A V1. Timber ribs shade the façade; water carries residual heat to the roof and returns after cooling (schematic illustration).}
\label{fig:conceptA}
\end{figure}

Cactus ribs \cite{lewis1977} inspire shading while the blood flow to jackrabbit ears
inspires heat transport to a separate exchange surface \cite{asknature_jackrabbit}. The
pipes and thermosiphon are conventional engineering choices: warmer water can rise and
cooler water return if buoyancy overcomes pipe resistance \cite{akhter2021}.

\subsubsection{Concept B V1 --- Roughness Spray Retrofit}
\label{sec:conceptB}

\textbf{B}~V1 is a retrofit coating for an existing wall. Elephant hairs suggest small
heat-transfer structures, but work as conductive fins \cite{myhrvold2012}. The spray
instead aims to disturb warm near-wall air (Figure~\ref{fig:roughness}). Its benefit
depends on texture size, spacing, conductivity and local wind.

\subsubsection{Critical Engineering Questions}
\label{sec:ceq}

Q1: Can buoyancy circulate enough water? Q2: Can the roof release the collected heat?
Q3: Does the spray improve heat transfer under weak airflow? These questions test the
assumptions most likely to change concept selection.

\subsubsection{Engineering Check 1 --- Circulation and Pipe Size}
\label{sec:check1}

The first estimate applies \textbf{A}~V1 to a hypothetical new building with the
dimensions of the Modellierungsgebiet~03 reference block. Table~\ref{tab:assumptions}
lists the model inputs and the engineering assumptions made by the project team.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\caption{Assumptions for the circulation estimate.}
\label{tab:assumptions}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{L{3.2cm} Y}
\toprule
\textbf{Input} & \textbf{Value and basis} \\
\midrule
Building and façade & $64 \times 58$\,m outer plan; $42 \times 36$\,m courtyard; eaves at 15.4\,m and roof rise 4\,m. Two faces, assumed 65\,\% opaque: Area $= (64 + 58) \times 15.4 \times 0.65 \approx 1{,}220$\,m\textsuperscript{2}. \\
Collected heat & Assume $q'' = 100$\,W/m\textsuperscript{2} enters the water after shading; test 50--150\,W/m\textsuperscript{2}. Collection depends on panel-to-pipe contact. \\

Water near 30\,°C & Density $\rho \approx 996$\,kg/m\textsuperscript{3}; heat capacity $c_p \approx 4{,}180$\,J/(kg$\cdot$K); expansion coefficient $\beta \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-4}$\,K$^{-1}$ \cite[Table~3]{harvey1998}. Assume viscosity $\mu = 0.8$\,mPa$\cdot$s. \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

The chosen water-temperature differences, 10\,°C and 3\,°C, test stronger and weaker
driving conditions; they are not measured site values or comfort targets. A smaller
difference weakens buoyancy while requiring more flow to carry the same heat load.

Heat rate $\dot Q$ is energy carried per second; $\dot m$ is water mass flow. Assume
steady liquid flow and approximate the hot/cold leg difference by the water temperature
change $\Delta T$. Equations use kelvin (K): a difference of 10\,K equals a change of
10\,°C.

\begin{align}
\dot Q &= A_f\, q'' \approx 1{,}220\ \text{m}^2 \times 100\ \text{W/m}^2 = 122\ \text{kW} \\
\dot m &= \frac{\dot Q}{c_p\, \Delta T}\,; \qquad
\Delta p_b \approx \rho\, g\, H\, \beta\, \Delta T \\
\Delta p_{\text{loss}} &= \left(f \frac{L}{D} + \Sigma K\right) \frac{\rho v^2}{2}\,; \qquad
v = \frac{4 \dot m}{\rho \pi D^2}
\end{align}

Here $D$ is internal diameter, $v$ water speed and $g = 9.81$\,m/s\textsuperscript{2}.
Use Darcy friction factor $f = 0.3164/\mathrm{Re}^{0.25}$, with
$\mathrm{Re} = \rho v D/\mu$, and solve where friction equals buoyancy \cite{akhter2021}
(Table~\ref{tab:circulation}). $\mathrm{Re} \approx 26{,}000$--$39{,}000$ supports the
smooth-pipe turbulent-flow approximation.

\centering\small
\caption{Circulation estimates for the 122\,kW load.}
\label{tab:circulation}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{l l l l}
\toprule
\textbf{Water difference} & \textbf{Buoyancy pressure} & \textbf{Water flow} & \textbf{Internal diameter} \\
\midrule
10\,°C (= 10\,K) & $\approx$300\,Pa & 2.9\,kg/s & $\approx$180\,mm \\

Reducing the temperature difference more than doubles the required main-pipe diameter.
These estimates use most of the available buoyancy pressure before radiator losses are
included. \textbf{A} therefore needs less connected façade area, larger mains or shorter
parallel circuits. Varying the assumed heat input from 50 to 150\,W/m\textsuperscript{2}
gives a load of 61--183\,kW.

\subsubsection{Engineering Check 2 --- Roof Heat Release}
\label{sec:check2}

In steady operation, the roof must release the heat collected by the façade. Raman et
al.\ \cite{raman2014} measured 40.1\,W/m\textsuperscript{2} net cooling at air
temperature under sunlight above 850\,W/m\textsuperscript{2}. This specialised emitter
provides a benchmark, not an assigned property of our coating.

\begin{equation}
A_{\text{release}} = \frac{\dot Q}{q''_{\text{net}}}
\end{equation}

For the engineering estimate, assume a 35\,°C roof, 30\,°C air, effective sky temperature
20\,°C, solar reflectance $r = 0.95$, thermal emittance $\varepsilon = 0.90$, sunlight
$G = 800$\,W/m\textsuperscript{2} and convection coefficient $h = 3$--8\,W/(m\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$K).
Effective sky temperature represents incoming infrared radiation, not air temperature.
Neglect back-surface exchange and assume an unobstructed sky:

\begin{equation}
q''_{\text{net}} = h\,(T_{\text{roof}} - T_{\text{air}})
 + \varepsilon\sigma\,(T_{\text{roof}}^4 - T_{\text{sky}}^4) - (1 - r)\,G
\end{equation}

The terms represent heat to air, infrared heat exchange and absorbed sunlight. Fourth
powers require absolute temperature: $T\,[\text{K}] = T\,[^\circ\text{C}] + 273.15$; thus
35\,°C $= 308.15$\,K. With $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$\,W/(m\textsuperscript{2}$\cdot$K\textsuperscript{4}),
net release is $15$--$40 + 83 - 40 \approx 58$--83\,W/m\textsuperscript{2}
(Table~\ref{tab:roofarea}). Roof tilt and nearby buildings would change this estimate.

The model allocates about 740\,m\textsuperscript{2} to cooling: 168\,m total strip width
$\times$ 4.39\,m combined slope depth. Solar panels, rooflights and gaps limit this area
(Figure~\ref{fig:roof}). The first two cases give about 30--62\,kW, supporting roughly
300--620\,m\textsuperscript{2} of façade at 100\,W/m\textsuperscript{2}. A warmer sky
reduces radiative heat loss; near-zero net output makes the required area highly
sensitive. The connected façade area must therefore be reduced. Heat released to air
remains local heat redistribution.
