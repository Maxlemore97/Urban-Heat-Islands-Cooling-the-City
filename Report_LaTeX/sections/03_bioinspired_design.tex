\section{Bio-Inspired Design Process}

The chain this section has to make visible is
challenge $\rightarrow$ function $\rightarrow$ biology $\rightarrow$ abstraction
$\rightarrow$ concept $\rightarrow$ engineering check $\rightarrow$ evaluation
$\rightarrow$ iteration. Section~2 delivered the first two links. This section
delivers the biology and the abstraction, and hands a set of transferable
principles to the concept development in Section~\ref{sec:creating}.

F7 is worth noting: it is the only one of our functions that does not sit in a
single branch of the taxonomy. It is the \emph{pairing} of \enquote{sense
signals: temperature} with \enquote{process signals: respond to signals}, which
is the same construction the Life's Principles checklist uses when it asks that
\enquote{the signal it sends, the antennae it uses to detect the signal, and its
response match} \parencite{baumeister2014}. That the central function of our
design question has to be assembled from two branches is itself a finding: it is
why searching by organism would not have produced it.

\paragraph{Sources, in the order they were used.} AskNature strategies and
innovations to find candidates \parencite{asknature}; the \emph{Genius of Biome}
report for the temperate broadleaf forest, the biome Zurich lies in, for models
already translated to the built environment \parencite{hok2013}; primary
literature for the mechanisms; and commercial products where the transfer has
already been made, treated as state of the art rather than as models. AskNature
and AI-assisted search were used to \emph{find} candidates only. Every mechanism
claim is referenced --- but not yet uniformly to a primary paper: ten of the
twenty-one entries in Table~\ref{tab:models} still rest on an AskNature page
alone. That is a stated shortfall rather than a claimed standard, and the open
item below lists the replacements already identified.

\paragraph{The inverse question.} The method also asks what nature would
\emph{not} do, and the answers narrowed the search as effectively as the
positive ones. Nature would not use a mirror finish, would not run a compressor,
would not pump drinking water onto a wall, and would not build one fixed
response for both January and July. Three of those four are constraints in
Table~\ref{tab:constraints}; the fourth is the seasonal problem this project
does not yet solve.

\paragraph{Which lenses were applied.} Of the five lenses in the method, two did
most of the work. The \emph{operating-condition lens} — intense solar radiation,
surfaces above \qty{60}{\celsius}, freeze--thaw, weeks without rain — led to the
desert organisms. The \emph{local lens} led to \parencite{hok2013}, because
Zurich sits in the temperate broadleaf forest biome and that report translates
exactly this biome into building design. The \emph{function lens} produced
Table~\ref{tab:searchpath}. The naturalist and ecological lenses contributed
context but no model that survived selection.

\paragraph{The five rejections, and why they matter.} A search is only legible
if what it discarded is on the record. The \emph{termite mound}, the standard
reference for passive cooling, was de-selected because the chimney account is
contested: measurements support wind-driven transient gas exchange rather than a
thermosiphon, and the building usually cited for it uses fans
\parencite{turner2008}. The \emph{Namib desert beetle}, the most-cited water
model, was de-selected because fog in Zurich is a winter phenomenon and a heat
wave brings neither rain nor fog. \emph{Thomson's gazelle} carotid rete was
de-selected as a whole-organism model --- it is internal physiology --- though
the counter-current \emph{arrangement} is retained as an option for the liquid
loop of Section~\ref{sec:concepta}. \emph{Tenrec aestivation} was de-selected
because it is a behaviour, and an envelope has none. \emph{Green façades and
roofs} were placed out of scope: they are already the client's HA~09 and HA~10,
so they are the baseline rather than our contribution.


Two features of the comparison are deliberate. First, the same organism appears
three times --- the elephant supplies ear-based transport, boundary-layer
hairs and localised hot spots, and these are three different mechanisms serving
three different functions, not one model counted three times. Second, the rejections above use
one stated criterion rather than case-by-case taste, and it is worth stating
explicitly because it explains an asymmetry in what survived: most cooling in
nature is metabolic or behavioural, and an envelope has neither metabolism nor
behaviour. The two models that transfer most directly --- the snail shell and the
cactus rib --- are the two that are \emph{structures rather than processes}.

\subsubsection{Mechanisms in depth}

Two mechanisms are developed further here. They were chosen because each answers
a question Section~2 left open; a third, the stomatal valve, is taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:creating}, where it becomes a vent rather than a pore.

\paragraph{Prevention --- why reflection alone is not the strategy.} The desert
snail survives on sun-exposed ground at up to \qty{50}{\celsius} without moving,
without water, and without a metabolism that could help. Its shell reflects
about \qty{90}{\percent} of visible and about \qty{95}{\percent} of near-infrared
light, which is above the $\alpha_\mathrm{sol}\geq0.80$ that KPI~2 asks for and
close to the ultra-white laboratory paints of \textcite{li2021} --- achieved with
calcium carbonate rather than a manufactured pigment. The part that matters for
us is what happens next. In extreme conditions the animal seals the aperture and
retracts into the second whorl, so an air space separates its tissue from the
shell wall nearest the hot ground. Reflection reduces what is absorbed; the air
space stops what is still absorbed from reaching what must be protected. For a
retrofit this maps onto a construction that already exists: a reflective outer
skin in front of a ventilated cavity. That matters for the night window, because
the \qty{7}{\kelvin} the city loses at night is heat released from mass that was
charged during the day, and a decoupled outer skin charges far less of it.

\paragraph{Regulation --- how a switch can behave like a controller.} In a
honeybee colony the temperature at which an individual worker begins to fan
varies between individuals, and the variation is genetic: a colony has one
queen but many fathers, so it contains patrilines with different thresholds. As
the nest warms, first a few bees fan, then more, then many, and the nest is held
below about \qty{36}{\celsius} with no central control at all. Each bee is a
binary switch; the colony response is continuous. This resolves an objection
that would otherwise disqualify every switching material considered in
Section~\ref{sec:creating}: a single threshold gives an almost infinite
$\partial P/\partial T_s$ at the switching point and roughly zero either side of
it, which is not the graded response KPI~1 asks for. A \emph{distribution} of
thresholds across many small units restores it. The same pattern appears in
\textcite{hok2013} as the \enquote{lots of littles} principle, arrived at
independently.

\paragraph{One principle, found three times.} The jackrabbit and the elephant
regulate by how much \emph{area} is active, the bees by how many \emph{units}
have switched, and a bilayer shading element by how far it has \emph{deployed}.
All three modulate how many elements act rather than how strongly each one acts,
and all three arrive at a graded response without a controller. That convergence
from three unrelated organisms is the strongest abstraction this project has
produced, and it is the one carried into every concept in
Section~\ref{sec:creating}.

\subsubsection{Life's Principles evident in the selected strategies}

The principles below are the ones the \emph{selected models} actually exhibit;
Section~\ref{sec:sustainability} assesses how well our final concept applies
them, which is a different question.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Be locally attuned and responsive} --- present in every model
        that couples its response to the local load, and named by the checklist
        in the form our F7 requires: the signal, the antenna that detects it and
        the response must match \parencite{baumeister2014}.
  \item \textbf{Be resource efficient (material and energy)}, sub-principle
        \emph{use low energy processes} --- none of the selected mechanisms
        consumes energy; the load itself drives them.
  \item \textbf{Adapt to changing conditions}, sub-principles \emph{incorporate
        diversity} and \emph{maintain integrity through self-renewal} ---
        \emph{Encelia} and the pine cone for the first, the lichen for the
        second.
  \item \textbf{Use life-friendly chemistry} --- \emph{Cyphochilus} achieves
        with geometry what industry achieves with titanium dioxide. The lichen is
        the more careful case: its water repellency does involve a compound, but
        that compound works \emph{with} a multi-scale geometry rather than
        instead of it, and nothing is released to do the job.
\end{itemize}

Three combinations were read off Table~\ref{tab:morph} and checked for physical
compatibility before any was developed further.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Water façade.} Ribbed self-shading skin (F2, F3) $+$ thermal mass
        carried in a liquid (F6) $+$ a buoyancy-driven loop to a radiating
        surface with a good sky view (F4, F5). Self-regulation comes from the
        loop itself.
  \item \textbf{Roughness spray.} Bright finish (F2) $+$ boundary-layer texture
        for convection (F5) and nothing else. Self-regulation comes from the
        convective term.
  \item \textbf{Switching coating.} Thermochromic finish that darkens in winter
        and brightens in summer (F2) $+$ no removal path.
        \textbf{Dropped:} it acts only on what is absorbed, so it has no effect
        in the night window --- which Section~\ref{sec:designquestion} identifies
        as the larger half of the problem and the half the incumbent already
        fails. It would have reproduced HA~11 with extra chemistry.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Combinations~1 and~2 were carried forward. They are separated by
physics rather than by styling: the first \emph{moves} heat to a place where it
leaves more easily, the second lets moving air reach the surface where the heat
already is. They also address different building stocks --- the first needs
pipework inside the construction and is a new-build system, the second is
sprayed onto what already stands. The retrofit constraint in
Table~\ref{tab:constraints} is soft, which is what makes this pair admissible.

\paragraph{How biology was balanced against feasibility.} Nothing in either
concept reproduces a biological \emph{form}. The elephant's ear contributes the
\emph{arrangement} --- absorb in one place, release in another, and let the
transport track the load --- not a shape; the pipe network is conventional
building services, and only its \emph{pumplessness} is biomimetic. The cactus
contributes a folded profile, which is manufactured as extruded or cast relief
rather than as a plant silhouette. The elephant's hair contributes a length
scale relative to the boundary layer, which becomes a sprayed aggregate, not a
bristle. Where the biology offered something we could not build --- vasodilation
as an actively controlled valve --- we kept the principle and dropped the
actuation.

\subsubsection{Concept A --- the water façade}
\label{sec:concepta}

\paragraph{What it is.} A new-build façade whose outer skin is a ribbed,
light-coloured panel and whose build-up carries a closed water loop. The ribs
shade the troughs between them, as a cactus does, so part of the surface is
never in direct sun. Behind the panel, wide-bore risers absorb what the surface
does take up. Water warmed in the risers becomes less dense and rises on its own;
cooled water in the return line sinks. At the top the loop opens into a thin,
wide plate at roof level, where the water is spread over the largest area the
roof allows and gives its heat to the night sky and to the air above the
building. Nothing is pumped, nothing is controlled and no water is consumed --- the
loop is closed.

\paragraph{Functional chain.} F2 and F3 at the ribbed surface reduce what is
absorbed; F6 stores what is absorbed in the water rather than in the masonry;
F5 and F4 release it at the roof plate; and F7 --- the coupling the design
question asks for --- is not an added device but the physics of the loop itself.
The warmer the façade, the larger the density difference between riser and
return, the larger the driving head, and the faster the circulation. The
response scales with the load without a sensor.

\subsubsection{Concept B --- the roughness spray}
\label{sec:conceptb}

\paragraph{What it is.} A sprayable coating carrying a millimetre-scale
aggregate, applied to an existing bright façade. The particles stand proud of
the surface far enough to reach through the layer of still air that clings to any
smooth wall, so that moving canyon air touches the surface instead of gliding
over an insulating film. It adds no moving parts, no water and no construction;
it is applied by ordinary trades, is close to invisible from the street, and is
renewed by spraying again.

\paragraph{Functional chain.} F2 at the bright finish, F5 at the texture. There
is no F6 and no F4 beyond what the wall already does: B deliberately buys one
function well rather than four badly. F7 comes from the convective term itself
--- the flux is proportional to the surface-to-air difference, so the response
rises with the load, linearly rather than steeply.

\paragraph{Where the biology enters.} The elephant's sparse hairs sit \emph{below}
the density at which hair insulates, so each acts as a fin through the boundary
layer rather than as part of a blanket; the reported effect is an additional heat
loss of up to \qty{23}{\percent} \parencite{notredame_elephant}. The transferred
quantity is a \emph{length scale relative to the boundary layer}, not a bristle.
The lichen contributes the second requirement: multi-scale roughness that sheds
water and stays clean without a biocide \parencite[41--44]{hok2013}, which is
what keeps the texture from failing criterion~6. That this is manufacturable
rather than speculative is established by prior art: \emph{StoColor Lotusan}, a
micro-structured superhydrophobic façade paint modelled on the lotus leaf, has
been sold since 1999 and is stated by its manufacturer to resist algae and fungal
growth \emph{without biocide film protection} \parencite{sto_lotusan}.

\paragraph{The obvious objection.} Roughness can also \emph{thicken} the still
layer instead of piercing it, if the elements are too short or too dense. This
is the concept's decisive assumption and it is question~2 below.

        convective coefficient, or does it trap a deeper stagnant film and make
        things worse? This single question decides whether B works at all.
        \todo{Check by measurement: two identical plates, one sprayed, logged
        over a hot afternoon.}
  \item \textbf{Where does the heat actually go?} Is the roof area large enough
        to release what the façade collects? Checked below, and the answer
        changed the design.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Engineering check 1: does the loop drive itself?}

A thermosiphon circulates because the fluid in the warm leg is less dense than
in the cool leg. The available driving pressure over a loop of height $h$ is

\begin{equation}
\Delta p = \rho\, g\, \beta\, \Delta T\, h ,
\label{eq:thermosiphon}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\rho$ is the density of water, $g$ the acceleration of gravity,
$\beta$ its volumetric thermal expansion coefficient, $\Delta T$ the temperature
difference between riser and return, and $h$ the height between the collecting
and releasing sections.

\paragraph{Result.} Equation~\eqref{eq:thermosiphon} gives

\[
\Delta p = 995 \times 9.81 \times 3.0\times10^{-4} \times 10 \times 16
         \approx \qty{470}{\pascal} .
\]

\noindent The flux to be carried is
$\dot Q = \dot q A = \qty{100}{\watt\per\square\metre}\times\qty{288}{\square\metre}
\approx \qty{29}{\kilo\watt}$, which at $\Delta T = \qty{10}{\kelvin}$ requires a
mass flow of $\dot m = \dot Q/(c_p \Delta T) \approx \qty{0.7}{\kilo\gram\per\second}$.
Distributing that over a loop of roughly \qty{40}{\metre} developed length leaves
about \qty{12}{\pascal\per\metre} of allowable friction. A bore of
\qty{50}{\milli\metre} gives roughly \qty{34}{\pascal\per\metre} and fails; a
bore of about \qty{80}{\milli\metre} gives roughly \qty{4}{\pascal\per\metre}
and passes with margin for bends and the plate.

\paragraph{What this does and does not prove.} It establishes that the concept
does not need a pump, which is what the energy constraint demands, and it fixes
a design parameter that was previously free: the risers must be of the order of
\qty{80}{\milli\metre}, not the \qty{15}{\milli\metre} of ordinary heating
pipework. That is an architectural consequence --- it sets the build-up thickness
against the soft constraint in Table~\ref{tab:constraints}, and it is the reason
Concept~A is a new-build system. It does \emph{not} prove that the flow
distributes evenly across parallel risers, and it says nothing about winter.

\subsubsection{Engineering check 2: where does the heat go?}

The loop can only release what its releasing surface can pass. For a surface
close to ambient the net release is of the order of
\qtyrange{80}{100}{\watt\per\square\metre} at night, combining long-wave emission
to a clear sky with free convection \parencite{raman2014,zhai2017}. The required
area follows directly:

\begin{equation}
A_\mathrm{release} = \frac{\dot Q}{q''}
= \frac{\qty{29}{\kilo\watt}}{\qty{100}{\watt\per\square\metre}}
\approx \qty{290}{\square\metre} .
\label{eq:releasearea}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Result, and what it changed.} The roof of the reference block is
about $18 \times \qty{14}{\metre} = \qty{252}{\square\metre}$. The roof is
therefore \emph{too small} to release what one façade collects, before any
allowance for plant, access or photovoltaics. This is the most useful result in
this section, because it moved the problem: the binding limit on Concept~A is not
the driving pressure --- check~1 passes comfortably --- but the area available to
give the heat away. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} carries this into the selection,
and it is the weakness Concept~V2 answers.

\subsubsection{Selection and Final Concept V2}

\paragraph{Selected: a hybrid, A with B applied to it.} Neither concept is
carried forward alone. Engineering check~2 showed that the roof cannot release
what one façade collects --- \qty{290}{\square\metre} required against
\qty{252}{\square\metre} available --- so Concept~A as first drawn is limited not
by its physics but by the area of its one releasing surface. Concept~B raises the
release rate of \emph{any} surface it is sprayed on, at negligible cost and with
no construction.

\paragraph{What V2 changes.} The roof plate stops being the only radiator. In
V2 the ribbed façade panels are part of the return path, so the façade itself
releases at night what it collected by day, and the roughness spray is applied to
both the façade panels and the roof plate to raise the convective coefficient on
each. The releasing area is then the façade plus the roof rather than the roof
alone, which is what closes the gap check~2 opened. A second consequence follows
without being designed for: the spray is renewable, so the part of the system
that wears is also the part that can be replaced --- which repairs A's weakest
criterion.

\subsubsection{Remaining uncertainties}

Four are known and none is closed. \textbf{Does the texture pierce or thicken the
boundary layer?} B's decisive assumption is untested, and V2 now depends on it in

climate constraint requires operation to about \qty{-10}{\celsius}; freeze
protection without auxiliary energy is not designed. \textbf{Does the loop still

\qty{3}{\kelvin} the driving head falls to about \qty{140}{\pascal} and the
margin may vanish. \textbf{Does millimetre texture survive twenty years?} It sits
on the outside of the building, and criterion~6 asks for
