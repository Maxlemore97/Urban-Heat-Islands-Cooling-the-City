\subsubsection{Research path}
\label{sec:researchpath}

To find suitable solutions both the AskNature website as well as the AskNature chat
were used. The following keywords were used to find solutions produced in the
nature: cooling, thermal transfer, shading, heat reduction, reflect, evaporation.
Additionally the biologized questions were used to search for solutions using
search engines such as Google.

The models that were selected had to fulfill at least one function mentioned in
Section~\ref{sec:functions}. Additionally they had to fit into at least one of the
defined life principles. Table~\ref{tab:models} lists every candidate we looked at,
the nine models we kept and the four we dropped, with the reason for each decision.

\subsubsection{Natural Models}
\label{sec:models}

\paragraph{African Elephant: Ears}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:elephant_ears} African
elephants have large and highly vascularized ears which they use as heat exchange
surfaces. Warm blood is transported from the body core into the ear, where heat is
transferred to the surrounding air. By flapping their ears additional airflow is
created which further increases the heat transfer~\cite{asknature_ears}. This same
approach is also used by the jackrabbit~\cite{asknature_jackrabbit}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/image1.jpg}
\caption{African Elephant ears. Source: \todo{image source}.}
\label{fig:elephant_ears}
\end{figure}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Transfer thermal energy from a heat generating
region to a large, exchange surface and increase airflow across this surface.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Use dedicated heat rejection surfaces
in locations with favorable airflow to transfer and dissipate heat from urban
structures.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions
\end{minipage}\par

\paragraph{Elephant Skin: Sparse Hairs}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} African elephants have additionally to their ears a
second cooling mechanism. They have very sparsely spread hair all over their body.
Due to the low density of the body hair it does not act as an insulation layer but
rather as additional surface area to transfer heat. As seen in
Figure~\ref{fig:elephant_hair} it also serves to break the stationary air
surrounding the skin. This allows for a better heat transfer; Myhrvold et al.\
measured up to \qty{23}{\percent} more heat loss at low wind
speeds~\cite{myhrvold2012}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/image2.jpg}
\caption{African elephant hair. Source: \todo{image source}.}
\label{fig:elephant_hair}
\end{figure}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Increase heat-transfer area by distributing sparse
bulges across a warm surface.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Use small surface structures on
façades or other heated urban surfaces to enhance passive heat transfer without
substantially increasing material use.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient
\end{minipage}\par

\paragraph{Cactus Ribs}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} Many cacti have ribbed surfaces consisting of
repeated peaks and troughs. The peaks shade parts of the troughs and therefore
reduce direct solar exposure. Air within the shaded troughs remains cooler. The
ribbed geometry also increases surface area and disrupts airflow around the plant,
similar to the elephant hair~\cite{lewis1977,asknature_cactus}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Use folded surface geometry to combine
self-shading, increased heat-transfer area and controlled interaction with airflow.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Integrate ribbed or folded geometries
into urban surfaces to reduce direct solar exposure while simultaneously promoting
passive heat dissipation.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient
\end{minipage}\par

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Saharan Silver Ant: Reflective Hairs}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} The Saharan silver ant is covered with densely
packed hairs that have a triangular cross-section and grooved surfaces. These hairs
reduce heat gain in two ways: they strongly reflect visible and near-infrared solar
radiation through total internal reflection and they increase emissivity in the
mid-infrared range, allowing the ant to radiate excess body heat more efficiently.
Shi et al.\ measured that ants with hairs stay about \qtyrange{5}{10}{\celsius}
cooler than ants whose hairs were removed~\cite{shi2015,asknature_anthair}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Use micro structured surfaces to reflect incoming
solar radiation while simultaneously enhancing thermal radiation away from the
surface.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Develop façade or roof surfaces with
micro structured geometries that minimize absorption in the visible and
near-infrared solar spectrum while maximizing heat emission in the mid-infrared.
This could reduce solar heat gain and improve passive radiative cooling without
requiring active energy input.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Adapt to changing conditions / Use life-friendly
chemistry
\end{minipage}\par

\paragraph{Desert Snail Shell}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} The desert snail \emph{Sphincterochila boissieri}
limits heat gain through a highly reflective shell and internal insulating air
spaces as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:snail}. Schmidt-Nielsen et al.\ measured
approximately \qty{90}{\percent} reflectance in the visible spectrum and about
\qty{95}{\percent} in the near-infrared range~\cite{schmidtnielsen1971}. The snail
also withdraws into the upper parts of its shell, while air-filled spaces and
limited contact with the hot substrate reduce conductive heat
transfer~\cite{asknature_snail}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/image3.jpg}
\caption{Desert Snail~\cite{asknature_snail}.}
\label{fig:snail}
\end{figure}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Reduce incoming radiant energy and interrupt
conductive heat transfer using reflective surfaces and stationary air layers.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Combine highly reflective external
surfaces with insulating or ventilated air cavities to limit solar heat storage in
roofs and façades.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions
\end{minipage}\par

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Morpho Butterfly Wing}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} Morpho butterflies generate their characteristic
color using transparent chitin-and-air structures at micro- and nanoscale. As seen
in Figure~\ref{fig:morpho}, ridges and cross-ribs interact with incoming light
through diffraction and interference, causing selected wavelengths to be strongly
reflected. The color therefore results primarily from physical structure rather
than pigmentation~\cite{asknature_morpho,giraldo2016}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/image4.jpg}
\caption{Reflection with nano structures~\cite{giraldo2016}.}
\label{fig:morpho}
\end{figure}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Control the spectral interaction between a surface
and incoming radiation through micro- and nanoscale geometry.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Engineer structured surfaces that
selectively manipulate incident solar radiation while maintaining desired optical
properties. The Morpho demonstrates structural color rather than near-infrared heat
rejection, and the cooling application requires separate verification.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient
\end{minipage}\par

\paragraph{Camel Fur and Sweat}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} Dromedary camels combine insulation with evaporative
cooling. Figure~\ref{fig:camel} displays how exactly this mechanism works. The sweat
glands release water which removes the body heat while evaporation and the thick
fur reduces the heat that reaches the skin, so that less sweat is
needed~\cite{schmidtnielsen1957}. Furthermore the camel reflects light with their
pale color~\cite{asknature_camel}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/image5.jpg}
\caption{Insulation with evaporative cooling~\cite{asknature_camel}.}
\label{fig:camel}
\end{figure}

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Block heat using insulation in combination with
evaporation as a means of heat dissipation.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Combine shading or thermal insulation
with controlled evaporative cooling so that less water is required to achieve the
desired cooling effect.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions /
Use life-friendly chemistry
\end{minipage}\par

\needspace{9\baselineskip}
\paragraph{Pine Cone Scales}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} Pine cone scales respond to humidity despite
containing no muscles or active control system. Their layered structure contains
materials with different swelling behavior. When moisture is absorbed, one layer
expands more than the other, causing the scale to bend and close. As the scale
dries, it returns towards its open state. The movement is
reversible~\cite{dawson1997}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Convert an environmental change directly into
mechanical movement through differential material expansion.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Use environmentally responsive
material layers to create self-actuating shading or ventilation elements that
operate without motors or electronic control.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Adapt to changing conditions / Use life-friendly
chemistry
\end{minipage}\par

\paragraph{Honeybee Fanning Thresholds}

\textbf{Biological mechanism.} Western honeybee colonies regulate nest temperature
through the combined behavior of many workers. Individual bees have different
temperature-response thresholds~\cite{jones2004}. As nest temperature increases,
progressively more bees begin ventilating the hive. This distributed response
prevents excessive system-wide reactions to small temperature fluctuations and
stabilizes the nest with no central control~\cite{peters2019}.

\par\noindent\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}\setlength{\parindent}{15pt}
\hspace{15pt}\textbf{Functional abstraction.} Distribute different activation thresholds across
multiple independent elements to create a gradual system-level response.

\textbf{Design principle for urban cooling.} Use multiple passive cooling elements
with different trigger conditions so that cooling capacity increases progressively
with thermal load instead of operating through a single on/off threshold.

\textbf{Life's Principle:} Be resource efficient / Adapt to changing conditions
\end{minipage}\par
